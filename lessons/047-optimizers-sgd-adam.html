Optimizers: SGD → Adam

You can explain what momentum, RMSProp and Adam each add, and choose a learning rate for a new run.

Neural NetworksTraining Deep Nets

Why this matters— optimization

Backpropagation hands you a gradient: numbers saying which way each weight should move. It says nothing about how far. That decision — the update rule — separates a model that reaches a good loss in an hour from one that oscillates, crawls, or diverges. Every optimizer from plain SGD to Adam is the same loop with a smarter answer to "how big a step, and in which direction?"

The failure it prevents. An exam asks why Adam converges faster than SGD on a sparse embedding layer. "Adam is adaptive" is a label; the marks come from naming the per-coordinate scaling by a running average of squared gradients. In practice the same gap is a training curve that falls for ten steps then flatlines too high, because the rate was copied from another architecture.

The one core idea— optimization

All four optimizers compute the same thing and differ in how they smooth it. Write θ(theta) (theta) for the parameters, g for the gradient of the loss with respect to θ, and η(eta) (eta) for the learning rate. Plain gradient descent does θ ← θ − η·g. Momentum averages recent gradients, so a persistent direction is amplified and a flip-flopping one cancels. RMSProp divides each coordinate by the typical size of its own gradients, so a weight with tiny gradients still moves. Adam does both, with a correction for averages that start at zero.

In one line: the gradient gives the direction; the optimizer decides how much history to remember and whether to trust each coordinate equally. Adam's defaults are η = 3×10⁻⁴, β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸ — worth knowing by heart.

SGD: θ ← θ − ηg · Adam: per-coordinate, history-smoothed steps

Momentum smooths the direction; RMSProp normalizes the size; Adam combines them and corrects the startup bias.

How it actually works— optimization

Six parts: the objects, the plain step, momentum, RMSProp, Adam, and the practical settings.

Part 1 — Two lists of the same shape

θ is every learnable number in the model, flattened into one list; g has the same length, because each parameter gets its own derivative. The loss is a surface over that list — 1,000 parameters mean a 1,000-dimensional surface nobody can picture, which every optimizer treats identically.

Why this part exists: gradients are per parameter, so "the learning rate is too high" means "too high for some coordinates" — exactly what adaptive methods exploit.

Part 2 — Plain SGD and the learning rate

SGD takes the step θ ← θ − η·g and repeats. The batch is a sample, so g is noisy; smaller batches need smaller steps. The surface is usually a narrow valley: the gradient across it is large and alternates sign, while the gradient along it is small and consistent.

Why this part exists: it is the baseline for every other optimizer, and it explains the zigzag — one step size cannot suit both axes.

Part 3 — Momentum remembers direction

Keep a velocity v the same shape as θ and update it with v ← β₁·v + g, then step θ ← θ − η·v. With β₁ = 0.9, the velocity is roughly a weighted average of the last 10 gradients: consistent directions add up, alternating ones cancel.

Why this part exists: it fixes oscillation without changing the step size, which is why momentum is the cheapest upgrade to SGD.

Part 4 — RMSProp rescales each coordinate

Keep a second average s ← β₂·s + (1 − β₂)·g², elementwise, and step with θ ← θ − η·g / (√s + ε)(square root). A coordinate whose gradients are typically 0.01 is divided by 0.1, so its effective step is ten times larger; one with gradients of 10 is divided by 10.

Why this part exists: embeddings and biases have gradients far smaller than dense weights, and one global rate cannot serve both.

Part 5 — Adam combines them, then corrects the start

Adam tracks both averages: m for the gradient and v for its square. Because both start at zero, early estimates are biased low, so it divides by 1 − β₁ᵗ and 1 − β₂ᵗ at step t. The update is θ ← θ − η·m̂ / (√v̂ + ε), with hats for the corrected averages.

Why this part exists: without correction the first step is about ten times too small; with it, the first step is roughly η times the sign of the gradient.

Part 6 — Weight decay and the knobs you actually set

L2 regularization adds λθ(lambda) to the gradient, which Adam scales by 1/√v̂, so heavily updated weights decay less. AdamW decouples it: shrink θ by η·λ, then apply the Adam step. Set η first, add warmup for transformers, and clip gradient norm to 1.0.

Why this part exists: these settings cause most "Adam does not generalize" reports.

The velocity carried 0.8 units of leftward motion, and one rightward gradient could not reverse it. A one-step reversal is treated as noise, not a new direction — which also means momentum can overshoot.

Step 1, in full. m = 0.9(0) + 0.1(2) = 0.2, v = 0.999(0) + 0.001(4) = 0.004. Correct the start: m̂ = 0.2/0.1 = 2.0, v̂ = 0.004/0.001 = 4.0. The step is 0.1 × 2.0/(√4.0 + ε) = 0.1, so w = 0.9. Without correction it would be 0.1 × 0.2/0.0632 = 0.316 — three times too large.

Step 2. m = 0.9(0.2) + 0.1(−1) = 0.08, v = 0.999(0.004) + 0.001(1) = 0.004996. Correct with 1 − 0.9² = 0.19 and 1 − 0.999² = 0.001999: m̂ = 0.421, v̂ = 2.499, so the step is 0.0266 and w = 0.873. The gradient flipped and the step shrank to a quarter — damping driven by gradient size rather than direction.

The trap— optimization

Most common wrong mental model

"Adam is the best optimizer, so switch to it and stop tuning the learning rate." Adam is the most forgiving optimizer at the start, not the best at the end. Its per-coordinate scaling can fit training data faster while landing in a flatter, worse-generalizing minimum than tuned SGD with momentum; and because it normalizes by gradient size, the learning rate still sets every step. Leaving η = 0.001 because "that is Adam's default" is still a choice.

It feels right because Adam's loss curve is smoother and reaches low training loss quickly. The validation curve decides, and it often separates later.

Correction: treat the optimizer as two decisions — direction smoothing and step size. Pick AdamW for transformers, tune η over a factor of ten, add warmup, and compare against SGD with momentum on validation before claiming an improvement.

The tell: training loss falling while validation loss is flat or rising — that is capacity or regularization, not the optimizer. A second tell is divergence at η = 10⁻² with Adam but not at 10⁻³; adaptive scaling does not remove the step bound. A third is weight norms growing when you asked for decay.

Retrieval check— optimization

  1. What does momentum do to a gradient that alternates sign every step, and why?
    Answer

    It cancels it. The velocity averages recent gradients, so +g and −g contributions annihilate partly, leaving a small consistent step.

  2. Why does Adam divide by 1 − β₁ᵗ on step t?
    Answer

    Because m starts at zero, early values are biased low. Dividing by 1 − 0.9ᵗ rescales the estimate to the right magnitude.

  3. AdamW differs from Adam with L2 in what way?
    Answer

    It applies decay directly to the weights instead of adding it to the gradient, so Adam's denominator does not rescale it.

Solve it · 047 — Add Adam to the update loop

Build it in Colab— optimization

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. Sections, plots and line-by-line notes — run the cells in order, then change the learning rate and rerun.

import numpy as np
w, lr, b1, b2, eps = 1.0, 0.1, 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8
m = v = 0.0                                # Adam's two running averages
for t, g in enumerate([2.0, -1.0], start=1):
    m = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g              # smoothed gradient
    v = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g          # smoothed squared gradient
    mh, vh = m / (1 - b1**t), v / (1 - b2**t)
    w -= lr * mh / (np.sqrt(vh) + eps)     # the Adam step
    print(f"t={t} w={w:.3f}")
