Optimization failure modes

You can diagnose vanishing or exploding gradients and name the fix.

Why this matters

A gradient is a message travelling backwards from the loss to the first layer. Every hop multiplies it by one more factor, so a 40-layer network asks that message to survive 40 multiplications. When it does not arrive, the early layers never change and the model looks broken rather than badly tuned.

This is the difference between a deep network that trains like a shallow one and a deep network that simply sits there. Without these fixes, adding layers makes results worse and you conclude that depth does not work — when in fact depth works only when the signal reaches it. The exam form is a diagnosis question: "a 20-layer network's first five layers have gradients near 10⁻⁹; name the cause and the single most effective change."

The one core idea

Imagine a rumour passed along a line of ten people, each repeating it at 60% of the volume they heard. By the tenth person it is inaudible. Now imagine each of them repeats it at 150% volume: by the tenth it is a shout, and by the fiftieth it is a physical event. Backpropagation is that line of people.

In one line: a gradient's size is a product, so factors below 1 collapse and factors above 1 detonate. Ten layers at 0.25 leave 9.5 × 10⁻⁷ of the original signal — below float32 resolution. Ten layers at 1.5 give 58×, and the loss becomes NaN shortly after.

How it actually works

Every fix below does one of two things: it keeps the per-layer factor near 1, or it gives the gradient a shortcut that skips the multiplication altogether.

Part 1 — The chain rule multiplies one factor per layer

The gradient of the loss with respect to an early layer is the product of every later layer's local derivative. Ten layers give ten factors; fifty give fifty. Nothing in that path is added, so the result is only as healthy as the smallest factor in the chain.

Why this part exists: calling a gradient "a number the framework computes" hides the fact that doubling depth squares the problem rather than merely lengthening it.

Part 2 — Each activation caps its own factor

Sigmoid's derivative peaks at 0.25 and is far smaller near saturation. Ten sigmoid layers therefore multiply the signal by at most 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5 × 10⁻⁷. ReLU has derivative exactly 1 for any positive input, so it shrinks nothing at all.

Why this part exists: that one number, 0.25, is why "switch to ReLU" answers almost every vanishing-gradient question.

Part 3 — Initialisation holds the variance steady

He initialisation draws weights with standard deviation √(2 / fan_in); for 512 inputs that is √(2/512) = 0.0625. Xavier uses √(1 / fan_in) and suits tanh, which is centred at zero. The scale is chosen so activation variance stays roughly constant through the layer.

Why this part exists: a bad init sets the product at the wrong scale before training begins, and no number of steps reliably recovers from it.

Part 4 — Normalisation re-centres the operating point

BatchNorm and LayerNorm subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation of the activations, then apply a learned scale and shift. Every layer's output therefore arrives with mean 0 and variance 1. During training those statistics come from the batch; at inference, from stored running averages.

Why this part exists: saturation only hurts when the input has drifted into the flat region; re-centring keeps the derivative large.

Part 5 — Residual connections add an identity path

A residual block computes y = x + f(x), so its Jacobian is ∂y/∂x = I + ∂f/∂x. If the block's own derivative is 0.25, the total is 1.25, and ten blocks deliver 1.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.3 where a plain stack delivers 9.5 × 10⁻⁷. This is the idea that made 100-plus-layer networks trainable.

Why this part exists: it is the only fix here that changes the arithmetic of the product rather than the size of each factor.

Part 6 — Clipping bounds the step

Measure the gradient's norm and, if it exceeds a threshold such as 1.0, rescale the whole vector down to that threshold while keeping its direction. A gradient of [3, 4] has norm 5, so multiplying by 1/5 gives [0.6, 0.8], norm exactly 1.0.

Why this part exists: one outlier batch can otherwise throw the weights into a region where every activation saturates and no gradient ever returns.

Part 7 — Warmup ramps the learning rate

Start at a small learning rate, often 1/100 of the target, and raise it linearly over the first few hundred steps. Early gradients are large and the weights are still random, which makes step 1 the most dangerous step of the run.

Why this part exists: clipping bounds the gradient but not gradient times learning rate; warmup bounds the distance the weights actually travel.

Worked example

1. How fast the signal dies

Take one factor per layer and multiply it through. The table is just repeated multiplication; the point is how fast the two columns separate.

LayersGain 0.25Gain 1.5
59.8 × 10⁻⁴7.6
109.5 × 10⁻⁷58
508 × 10⁻³¹6.4 × 10⁸

What to notice. At layer 5 the shrinking column is already below 0.001, and by layer 30 it stands at 8.7 × 10⁻¹⁹ — far under the 10⁻⁷ relative precision of a float32 weight, so the update rounds to nothing. The right-hand column does the opposite: 1.5⁵ is a harmless 7.6, but fifty layers of it is 640 million, and the next step moves every weight by more than the weights themselves. No learning rate rescues both columns at once, because the problem is the exponent, not the step size.

2. What clipping does to one gradient

A gradient vector g = [3, 4] with threshold 1.0. The direction must survive; only the length changes.

StepArithmeticResult
Norm√(3² + 4²) = √255.0
Scale factor1.0 / 5.00.2
Clipped gradient0.2 × [3, 4][0.6, 0.8]
Check the norm√(0.36 + 0.64)1.0

What to notice. The ratio of the two components is unchanged — 3:4 before, 0.6:0.8 after — so clipping preserves the direction the gradient asked for and only caps how far it can travel. A gradient of norm 0.7 would pass through untouched, which is why clipping is safe to leave on permanently. What it cannot fix is a gradient that is consistently huge: that is an initialisation or learning-rate problem, and clipping only hides it.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"More depth is more power, so just add layers." It feels right because depth does buy representational power, and every headline model is deep. But the optimisation problem grows faster than the representation does, so an unassisted 50-layer network is usually worse than a 10-layer one — not because it cannot express the answer, but because it cannot find it.

Correction: depth is a claim you have to earn with He initialisation, normalisation and residual paths. Given those, 100 layers train. Without them, 20 layers can already be untrainable, and the failure looks identical to a learning rate that is too small.

Second trap: assuming BatchNorm behaves the same in training and inference. At inference it uses stored running statistics, not the statistics of the batch in front of it. Forget model.eval() and a single-image batch produces nonsense.

The tell: print the mean gradient norm of each layer, first to last, on one batch. If it falls by an order of magnitude per layer you have vanishing; if it doubles per layer you have exploding; if it is healthy but loss is flat, you have a different problem entirely. The second tell is a loss curve that is not smooth but stair-stepped upward in the first hundred steps — that is a warmup or clipping problem, not a data problem.

Retrieval check

  1. Why does y = x + f(x) help gradients reach layer 1?
    Answer

    ∂y/∂x = I + ∂f/∂x. The identity term carries the gradient through untouched, so it cannot be shrunk to zero by the block's Jacobian alone.

  2. Loss goes to NaN after 30 steps. Name the first two things you try.
    Answer

    Lower the learning rate by 10×, and clip the gradient norm (e.g. 1.0). Both target the same cause: a step so large it leaves the numerically stable region.

  3. A 20-layer sigmoid network's first five layers have gradients around 1e-9. Which single change helps most?
    Answer

    Replace sigmoid with ReLU (or GELU). Sigmoid's derivative is at most 0.25, so depth multiplies the gradient away; ReLU has derivative 1 for positive inputs.

# the fix: residual path keeps an identity term
