Optimization failure modes

You can diagnose vanishing or exploding gradients and name the fix.

Why this matters

A 40-layer network that trains like a 2-layer one is not magic; it is four specific engineering fixes. Without them, depth actively hurts, and the failure looks like "my model just doesn't learn".

The one core idea

Backprop multiplies Jacobians layer by layer. Products of numbers below 1 vanish; above 1 they explode. Every fix either keeps those factors near 1 or shortens the path the gradient travels.

How it actually works

  • Vanishing: sigmoid's derivative peaks at 0.25. Ten layers → 0.25¹⁰ ≈ 9.5 × 10⁻⁷. Early layers receive nothing and stop learning.
  • Exploding: weights with gain 1.5 over ten layers → 1.5¹⁰ ≈ 58; over fifty layers → 6 × 10⁸. Loss becomes NaN.
  • Initialisation: Xavier/Glorot for tanh, He for ReLU — scale initial weights so variance is preserved through the layer.
  • Normalisation: BatchNorm/LayerNorm re-centre and re-scale activations, keeping the operating point in the responsive range.
  • Residual connections: y = x + f(x) gives gradients an identity path straight to the input. This single idea is what made 100+ layer networks trainable.
  • Gradient clipping: rescale the gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold. The standard fix for exploding gradients in RNNs and RL.
  • Warmup: start with a tiny learning rate, ramp up. Prevents early divergence when initial gradients are large.

Worked example

1. How fast the signal dies

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"More depth is more power, so just add layers." Beyond a point, depth without residual connections and normalisation reduces trainability. The optimisation problem gets harder faster than the representation gets richer.

Second trap: assuming BatchNorm behaves the same in training and inference. At inference it uses stored running statistics, not batch statistics. Forget model.eval() and a batch of one produces nonsense.

Retrieval check

  1. Why does y = x + f(x) help gradients reach layer 1?
    Answer

    ∂y/∂x = I + ∂f/∂x. The identity term carries the gradient through untouched, so it cannot be shrunk to zero by the block's Jacobian alone.

  2. Loss goes to NaN after 30 steps. Name the first two things you try.
    Answer

    Lower the learning rate by 10×, and clip the gradient norm (e.g. 1.0). Both target the same cause: a step so large it leaves the numerically stable region.

  3. A 20-layer sigmoid network's first five layers have gradients around 1e-9. Which single change helps most?
    Answer

    Replace sigmoid with ReLU (or GELU). Sigmoid's derivative is at most 0.25, so depth multiplies the gradient away; ReLU has derivative 1 for positive inputs.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
for gain in (0.25, 1.0, 1.5):
    vals = gain ** np.arange(1, 51)
    print(f"gain {gain}: after 10={gain**10:.2e}  after 50={gain**50:.2e}")
# the fix: residual path keeps an identity term
for depth in (5, 50):
    jac = 0.25 ** depth
