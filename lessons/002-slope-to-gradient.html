Slope → gradient

You can compute a gradient and explain why training steps against it.

Why this matters

Every training loop in this course — backprop, Q-learning, PPO — is the same sentence: measure the slope, step the other way. PyTorch reduces it to two lines, loss.backward() then p ← p − η · p.grad, and every optimizer you meet is a variation on how far to trust the second one.

The failure this prevents is a run that looks alive and is not. On f(w) = w² with η = 10 instead of 0.1, one step takes w = 3 to 3 − 10(6) = −57 and the loss from 9 to 3,249, then keeps multiplying until it overflows; the traceback names a matrix multiply, never the learning rate. The same two lines settle the exam question "f(w) = 3w² at w = 2, one step at η = 0.1": the slope is 6w = 12, so w ← 2 − 1.2 = 0.8.

The one core idea

Stand on a hillside in fog. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under one boot. A gradient is that reading taken at every knob at once: one slope per parameter, each answering "if I turn this alone, how fast does the loss change?" A thousand knobs give a thousand slopes that still describe one direction — the steepest way up.

In one line: the gradient is the uphill direction, so learning is a subtraction, repeated. On f(w) = w² at w = 3 the slope is 2w = 6; with η = 0.1 the step is 3 − 0.6 = 2.4 and the loss falls 9 → 5.76. At w = 1 the slope is only 2, so the same η buys a quarter of the drop.

How it actually works

Seven parts. Each one fixes a problem the previous part leaves open.

Part 1 — A slope is a ratio of two changes

Slope is rise over run, Δf / Δw. Between w = 1 and w = 3 on f(w) = w² the output goes 1 → 9, so the average slope is 8 / 2 = 4. That describes the interval, not the point where a step begins. Why this part exists: an interval average can carry the wrong sign — fall then rise, and it reads near zero while the ground under your feet is steep.

Part 2 — Shrink the interval until the ratio settles

Pick a small h and compute (f(w+h) − f(w)) / h: at w = 3, h = 0.01 gives 6.01 and h = 0.001 gives 6.001. The sequence homes in on 6, the derivative, and for w² the algebra says the answer is 2w at every point. Why this part exists: stopping early ties the answer to an arbitrary h — h = 1 gives 7 — and that error would never wash out of the step size.

Part 3 — Freeze every other knob

A partial derivative ∂f/∂x nudges x with every other variable frozen. On f(x, y) = x² + 3y at (2, 1), ∂f/∂x = 2x = 4 and ∂f/∂y = 3; the second never shrinks, so no value of y settles there. Why this part exists: turn two knobs at once and the change belongs to neither, so you cannot tell which one to fix.

Part 4 — Stack the partials into one vector

The gradient ∇f stacks the partials in a fixed order: ∇f(2, 1) = (4, 3). One entry per parameter, in the same order as the parameter vector — topic 1's shape rule on a new object. Why this part exists: swap the entries and training improves the wrong parameter silently, with no error and no learning.

Part 5 — The vector points uphill

Its direction is the steepest increase available and its length is the rate along it. At (2, 1), (4, 3) means push x four units of slope for every three of y. Along the level curve f = 7 nothing changes, so the gradient is perpendicular to it, aimed straight uphill. Why this part exists: without that guarantee you would probe directions one at a time instead of taking the best one immediately.

Part 6 — Subtract it, scaled by a learning rate

The update is w ← w − η∇f, where the learning rate η is how far you trust the slope. For f(x, y) = x² + 3y at (2, 1) with η = 0.1: (2, 1) − 0.1(4, 3) = (1.6, 0.7). Why this part exists: adding climbs; and a step of 1 here lands symmetrically on the far side of the valley with the loss unchanged.

Part 7 — Repeat, and watch the slope rather than the loss

Training is part 6 in a loop. On w² each step multiplies the distance to zero by 1 − 2η; with η = 0.1 that is 0.8, giving 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536. Convergence is geometric and needs η < 1 here. Why this part exists: one step proves nothing — shrinking slopes mean convergence, constant means a runaway direction, growing means divergence, and the loss alone cannot separate "slow" from "no minimum".

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"The gradient points downhill." It feels right for two reasons: the algorithm is called descent, and the code contains a minus sign, so the minus gets attached to the gradient instead of to the step. The arrow in every diagram points at the valley, and that arrow is −∇f.

At w = 3 on w² the gradient is +6, and the loss is rising in that direction: add it with η = 0.1 and you land at 3.6 with loss 12.96.

Correction: ∇f is steepest ascent. The descent direction is −∇f, and the minus lives in the update rule, not in the vector.

The tell: the loss climbs from step one while the gradient magnitude shrinks — smooth, monotone, no oscillation, because you are walking uphill and the ground gets gentler. Compare example 3, where a too-large rate also raises the loss but flips the step's sign each row and grows. If flipping one character in the update fixes training, it was never the learning rate.

Retrieval check

  1. f(w) = 3w² at w = 2. Compute the gradient and one step with η = 0.1.
    Answer

    f′ = 6w = 12. w ← 2 − 0.1(12) = 0.8. Loss falls from 12 to 3(0.64) = 1.92.

  2. You have the gradient of the loss. Why subtract it rather than add it?
    Answer

    Because it points toward higher loss. Subtracting moves toward lower loss locally. The minus sign is the whole algorithm; everything else is choosing η.

  3. f(x, y) = x + y² at (1, 3). What is ∇f, and which parameter will keep moving forever?
    Answer

    ∇f = (1, 6). x keeps moving: f is linear in x, so the gradient never shrinks. y settles at 0, where its gradient vanishes.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
f = lambda w: w ** 2
grad = lambda w: 2 * w
w, lr = 3.0, 0.1
for step in range(4):
    print(f"step {step}: w={w:7.4f}  loss={f(w):8.4f}  grad={grad(w):7.4f}")
    w -= lr * grad(w)
