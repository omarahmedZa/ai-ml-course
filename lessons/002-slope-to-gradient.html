Slope → gradient

You can compute a gradient and explain why training steps against it.

FoundationsCalculus & Optimization

Why this matters— the gradient

Every training loop in this course — backprop, Q-learning, PPO — is the same sentence: measure the slope, step the other way. The loss you minimise is a function f of the parameters, and the slope is its derivative (the gradient) with respect to those parameters — how fast the loss changes when a knob turns. PyTorch reduces it to two lines, loss.backward() then p ← p − η · p.grad(eta), and every optimizer you meet is a variation on how far to trust the second one.

The failure this prevents is a run that looks alive and is not. On f(w) = w² with η = 10 instead of 0.1, one step takes w = 3 to 3 − 10(6) = −57 and the loss from 9 to 3,249, then keeps multiplying until it overflows; the traceback names a matrix multiply, never the learning rate. The same two lines settle the exam question "f(w) = 3w² at w = 2, one step at η = 0.1": the slope is 6w = 12, so w ← 2 − 1.2 = 0.8.

The one core idea— the gradient

Stand on a hillside in fog. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under one boot. A gradient is that reading taken at every knob at once: one slope per parameter, each answering "if I turn this alone, how fast does the loss change?" A thousand knobs give a thousand slopes that still describe one direction — the steepest way up.

In one line: the gradient is the uphill direction, so learning is a subtraction, repeated. On f(w) = w² at w = 3 the slope is 2w = 6; with η = 0.1 the step is 3 − 0.6 = 2.4 and the loss falls 9 → 5.76. At w = 1 the slope is only 2, so the same η buys a quarter of the drop.

w ← w − η∇f

Step against the uphill slope, scaled by the learning rate.

How it actually works— the gradient

Seven parts, and they form a chain rather than a list: each one fixes a gap the previous part leaves open. Read them as one story — you are standing at w = 3 on a bowl-shaped loss and you want the bottom; what do you need to know, in order? The table is the map, the seven subsections are the walk.

Part 1 — A slope is a ratio of two changes

Slope is rise over run: how far the output moves for each unit you move the input. As a formula that is Δf / Δw — one change divided by another.

Try it on f(w) = w². Move from w = 1 to w = 3. The output starts at 1² = 1 and ends at 3² = 9, so the rise is 8 while the run is 2, and the average slope is 8 / 2 = 4. A straight line drawn between those two points climbs 4 units for every 1 across — that is what the number means.

What that number is not: the slope of the curve itself. It is the slope of the chord spanning the whole interval. The curve is steeper at w = 3 than at w = 1, and one average smears the two together. Worse, an average can lie about direction — a curve that falls and then rises has a negative change and a positive one that partly cancel, so the chord reads near zero even where the ground under your feet is steep.

Why this part exists. It gives you the idea of a slope — two changes compared. It does not yet give you the slope at any one point, and a point is all a training step can use.

Part 2 — Shrink the interval until the ratio settles

To get the slope at one point, shrink the interval around it. Pick a tiny step h and measure the chord from w to w + h: (f(w+h) − f(w)) / h. As h shrinks, that chord rotates toward the tangent — the straight line touching the curve at w.

On f(w) = w² at w = 3: with h = 0.1 the slope is (9.61 − 9)/0.1 = 6.1; with h = 0.01 it is 6.01; with h = 0.001 it is 6.001. The sequence homes in on 6 without overshooting.

You do not have to keep shrinking forever. Expand the algebra once: (w+h)² − w² = 2wh + h². Divide by h and you get 2w + h. Let h go to zero and what is left is 2w. That limit is the derivative, written f′(w) — a formula, not a table: 2w gives 6 at w = 3, 2 at w = 1, and 0 at the bottom.

Why this part exists. The derivative is the slope under your feet, which is what a step needs. Stop early at h = 1 and you get 7 instead of 6 — a 17% error that would travel into every step size you choose.

Part 3 — Freeze every other knob

A real model has many parameters, so "the slope" has to split: one slope per parameter. The partial derivative ∂f/∂x(partial) is the slope in x while every other variable is held still. The curly ∂ is the same idea as d, plus a reminder: other knobs frozen.

On f(x, y) = x² + 3y at (2, 1): freeze y and the second term acts as a constant, so along x the function looks like x² and ∂f/∂x = 2x = 4. Freeze x and the first term is the constant, so along y it looks like 3y and ∂f/∂y = 3. Two questions, two answers, one measured at a time.

Notice what the 3 implies: it never shrinks as y changes. The function is linear in y, so there is no bottom in that direction — no value of y makes its slope reach zero.

Why this part exists. Move two knobs at once and the change you observe belongs to neither: you cannot tell which one caused it, so you cannot correct either.

Part 4 — Stack the partials into one vector

Collect the partials in a fixed order and you have the gradient: ∇f(2, 1) = (4, 3). Entry one is always x, entry two is always y — the same order as the parameter vector from topic 1.

That order is not a convention you may shuffle. Swap the two numbers and the update turns the wrong knob by the right amount, silently: the shapes still match, nothing raises an error, and training learns the wrong parameter.

Why this part exists. A model can carry millions of parameters. The gradient keeps one number per knob in a single object, so one line of code can update all of them together.

Part 5 — The vector points uphill

A vector carries two facts: a direction and a length. The gradient uses both. Its direction is the steepest way up the surface; its length is how steep that climb is. At (2, 1) the vector (4, 3) reads as: for every 4 units of slope contributed by x, 3 come from y. Push along that mix and the loss rises faster than along any other mix of the two.

A second fact makes the vector useful: it is perpendicular to the level curve — the contour along which f does not change. Walking along that contour is wasted effort, since the loss stays at 7; the fastest way off it is straight across, and straight across is exactly where the gradient points.

Why this part exists. Without it you would probe directions one at a time, guessing. The gradient hands you the best direction at once, already measured.

Part 6 — Subtract it, scaled by a learning rate

The gradient points uphill and you want downhill, so the update goes against it: w ← w − η∇f. The learning rate η is how far you trust that reading — the length of the step.

At (2, 1) with η = 0.1: the step is 0.1 × (4, 3) = (0.4, 0.3), subtract it and you land at (1.6, 0.7). Check the loss: 1.6² + 3(0.7) = 2.56 + 2.1 = 4.66, down from 7. The step did exactly what the minus sign promised.

Why this part exists. Add instead of subtract and you climb. And η must be small enough: on f(w) = w² a rate of 1 sends w = 3 to −3, the far side of the valley at the same height 9 — no progress, just a mirror image.

Part 7 — Repeat, and watch the slope rather than the loss

One step is not training. Training is part 6 in a loop. On f(w) = w² the update becomes w ← w − η(2w) = w(1 − 2η). With η = 0.1 the bracket is 0.8, so w keeps 80% of its distance to zero at every step: 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536. That is geometric convergence — the gap shrinks by a constant factor, which is why the loss falls quickly at first and then crawls.

For this function the condition is η < 1. At η = 1 the bracket is −1, so w flips sign forever and the loss never moves. Above 1 the bracket is larger than 1 in size, so every step lands further away than the last.

Why this part exists. One step proves nothing, so watch the gradient from step to step. Shrinking slopes mean you are approaching a minimum; a slope that keeps the same size means you are on a slope, not in a bowl; a growing slope means you are diverging. The loss alone cannot separate "slow" from "no minimum" — the slope can.

The trap— the gradient

Most common wrong mental model

"The gradient points downhill." It feels right for two reasons: the algorithm is called descent, and the code contains a minus sign, so the minus gets attached to the gradient instead of to the step. The arrow in every diagram points at the valley, and that arrow is −∇f.

At w = 3 on w² the gradient is +6, and the loss is rising in that direction: add it with η = 0.1 and you land at 3.6 with loss 12.96.

Correction: ∇f is steepest ascent. The descent direction is −∇f, and the minus lives in the update rule, not in the vector.

What it feels likeWhat is true
The gradient points downhill.It points uphill; the update subtracts it.

The tell: the loss climbs from step one while the gradient magnitude shrinks — smooth, monotone, no oscillation, because you are walking uphill and the ground gets gentler. Compare example 3, where a too-large rate also raises the loss but flips the step's sign each row and grows. If flipping one character in the update fixes training, it was never the learning rate.

Retrieval check— the gradient

  1. f(w) = 3w² at w = 2. Compute the gradient and one step with η = 0.1.
    Answer

    f′ = 6w = 12. w ← 2 − 0.1(12) = 0.8. Loss falls from 12 to 3(0.64) = 1.92.

  2. You have the gradient of the loss. Why subtract it rather than add it?
    Answer

    Because it points toward higher loss. Subtracting moves toward lower loss locally. The minus sign is the whole algorithm; everything else is choosing η.

  3. f(x, y) = x + y² at (1, 3). What is ∇f, and which parameter will keep moving forever?
    Answer

    ∇f = (1, 6). x keeps moving: f is linear in x, so the gradient never shrinks. y settles at 0, where its gradient vanishes.
