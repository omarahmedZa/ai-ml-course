Slope → gradient

You can compute a gradient and explain why training steps against it.

FoundationsCalculus & Optimization

Why this matters— the gradient

Every training loop in this course — backprop, Q-learning, PPO — is the same sentence: measure the slope, step the other way. The loss you minimise is a function f of the parameters, and the gradient is its derivative: how fast the loss changes when a knob turns. PyTorch reduces it to two lines, loss.backward() then p ← p − η · p.grad(eta), and every optimizer you meet is a variation on how far to trust the second one.

The failure this prevents is a run that looks alive and is not. On f(w) = w² with η = 10 instead of 0.1, one step takes w = 3 to 3 − 10(6) = −57 and the loss from 9 to 3,249, then keeps multiplying until it overflows; the traceback names a matrix multiply, never the learning rate. The same two lines settle the exam question "f(w) = 3w² at w = 2, one step at η = 0.1": the slope is 6w = 12, so w ← 2 − 1.2 = 0.8.

The one core idea— the gradient

Stand on a hillside in fog. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground tilts under one boot. A gradient is that reading taken at every knob at once: one slope per parameter, each answering "if I turn this alone, how fast does the loss change?" A thousand knobs give a thousand slopes that still describe one direction — the steepest way up.

In one line: the gradient is the uphill direction, so learning is a subtraction, repeated. On f(w) = w² at w = 3 the slope is 2w = 6; with η = 0.1 the step is 3 − 0.6 = 2.4 and the loss falls 9 → 5.76. At w = 1 the slope is only 2, so the same η buys a quarter of the drop.

w ← w − η∇f

Step against the uphill slope, scaled by the learning rate.

How it actually works— the gradient

You are standing at w = 3 on f(w) = w², where the loss is 9, and you want the bottom. Seven steps, each ending with the gap the next one closes.

Step 1 — Measure a slope as two changes

Slope is rise over run, Δf / Δw(delta): one change divided by another. On f(w) = w², move w from 1 to 3 and the output goes from 1² = 1 to 3² = 9, so the slope is 8 / 2 = 4. That is the slope of the chord across the interval — one average, and not the slope under your feet at either end.

Step 2 — Shrink the interval until the ratio settles

Measure the chord from w to w + h and shrink h. At w = 3: h = 0.1 gives (9.61 − 9)/0.1 = 6.1, h = 0.01 gives 6.01, h = 0.001 gives 6.001 — homing in on 6. Algebra settles it: (w+h)² − w² = 2wh + h², divide by h for 2w + h, let h → 0 and the derivative f′(w) = 2w is left. Stop at h = 1 and you read 7 instead of 6 — 17% wrong.

Step 3 — Freeze every other knob

A model has many parameters, so the slope splits into one per knob: the partial derivative ∂f/∂x(partial), measured with the others held still. On f(x, y) = x² + 3y at (2, 1), freezing y leaves x², so ∂f/∂x = 2x = 4, and freezing x leaves 3y, so ∂f/∂y = 3. One number cannot describe a surface: move two knobs at once and the change you see belongs to neither.

Step 4 — Stack the partials into one vector

Collect the partials in a fixed order and you have the gradient: ∇f(2, 1) = (4, 3)(nabla), entry one always x, entry two always y, matching the parameter vector. The order may not be shuffled — swap the two numbers and the update turns the wrong knob by the right amount, silently. Two separate slopes are not enough because a million knobs must update in one line.

Step 5 — Read the vector as direction and length

A vector carries two facts, and the gradient uses both. Its direction is the steepest way up; its length is how steep that climb is. At (2, 1), (4, 3) says 4 units of rise per unit of x and 3 per unit of y, which climbs faster than any other mix. It is also perpendicular to the level curve where f stays at 7, and crossing that contour is the only way off it.

Step 6 — Subtract it, scaled by a learning rate

The gradient points uphill, so the update goes against it: w ← w − η∇f. The learning rate η(eta) is the length of the step. At (2, 1) with η = 0.1 the step is 0.1 × (4, 3) = (0.4, 0.3), landing at (1.6, 0.7), where the loss is 2.56 + 2.1 = 4.66, down from 7. Direction alone is not enough: at η = 1, 3 lands on −3, same height, no progress.

Step 7 — Repeat, and watch the slope

Training is step 6 repeated. On f(w) = w² the update becomes w ← w − η(2w) = w(1 − 2η); at η = 0.1 the factor is 0.8, so w keeps 80% of its distance each step: 3 → 2.4 → 1.92 → 1.536. That is geometric convergence. One step proves nothing, so track the slope: shrinking means a minimum is near, constant means you are on a slope rather than in a bowl, growing means divergence — and here that needs η < 1.

4. Check yourself

On paper first: reading the tables is not producing them.

  1. f(w) = w² at w = 1.5, η = 0.2. Where does one step land?
    Answer

    2(1.5) = 3, step 0.2 × 3 = 0.6, so w = 0.9 and the loss falls 2.25 → 0.81.

  2. ∇f(2, 1) = (4, 3), η = 0.1. Name the gradient, the step and the new point.
    Answer

    (4, 3) is the gradient, (0.4, 0.3) the step, (1.6, 0.7) the new point. Add the step instead and the loss rises from 7 to 5.76 + 3.9 = 9.66.

  3. The loss falls smoothly but |∇f| never shrinks. Where are you?
    Answer

    On a slope, not in a bowl: a bowl's gradient falls toward zero at the bottom, a constant one promises no minimum ahead.

The trap— the gradient

Most common wrong mental model

"The gradient points downhill." It feels right because the algorithm is called descent and the update carries a minus sign, so the minus gets attached to the gradient. But the arrow in every diagram points at the valley, and that arrow is −∇f.

At w = 3 on w² the gradient is +6, and the loss is rising in that direction: add it with η = 0.1 and you land at 3.6 with loss 12.96.

Correction: ∇f is steepest ascent. The descent direction is −∇f, and the minus lives in the update rule, not in the vector.

What it feels likeWhat is true
The gradient points downhill.It points uphill; the update subtracts it.

The tell: the loss climbs from step one while the gradient magnitude shrinks — smooth, monotone, no oscillation, because you are walking uphill and the ground gets gentler. Compare example 3, where a too-large rate also raises the loss but flips the sign each row and grows. If flipping one character in the update fixes training, it was never the learning rate.

Retrieval check— the gradient

  1. f(w) = 3w² at w = 2. Compute the gradient and one step with η = 0.1.
    Answer

    f′ = 6w = 12. w ← 2 − 0.1(12) = 0.8. Loss falls from 12 to 3(0.64) = 1.92.

  2. You have the gradient of the loss. Why subtract it rather than add it?
    Answer

    It points toward higher loss, so subtracting moves locally downhill. The minus sign is the whole algorithm; the rest is choosing η.

  3. f(x, y) = x + y² at (1, 3). What is ∇f, and which parameter will keep moving forever?
    Answer

    ∇f = (1, 6). x keeps moving: f is linear in x, so the gradient never shrinks. y settles at 0, where its gradient vanishes.
