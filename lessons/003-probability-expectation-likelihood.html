Probability, expectation, likelihood

You can explain why a loss is a negative log-likelihood, and read mean and variance off a distribution.

Why this matters

A classifier does not output a label; it outputs a distribution over labels, and that distribution is what gets scored. Softmax turns scores into probabilities, cross-entropy turns the probability of the true class into a loss, and both are likelihood arithmetic. The same move reappears in a language model (probability of the next token) and in RL (expected return over a policy).

The failure this prevents is quiet. Compare total NLL on two test sets — 500 rows and 2,000 rows — and the larger set wins by default, because −Σ log p is a sum that grows with the row count. A team reporting the sum can "improve" a model by evaluating it on more data. Dividing by N makes the number per-row and comparable, which is the difference between a metric and a mood.

The one core idea

A model is a bookmaker. Before the result is known it quotes a probability for each outcome; afterwards you score the quote by how much probability it gave to what actually happened. A quote of 0.9 on the winner is good, 0.01 is embarrassing, and the score is just −log of the quoted probability. Probability asks what will happen; likelihood asks how good this quote was.

In one line: loss is surprise, and surprise is −log p. A model that gave the truth 0.9 pays 0.105; one that gave it 0.1 pays 2.303; one that gave it 0.01 pays 4.605. Being confidently wrong costs about 44 times more than being confidently right, which is why the penalty tracks overconfidence rather than mere error.

How it actually works

Seven parts. Each one closes a gap the previous part leaves open.

Part 1 — A distribution is a list that sums to 1

A distribution gives each outcome a probability: a fair die puts 1/6 ≈ 0.167 on each of six faces, and the six numbers add to 1. That sum is what makes a probability a share of all outcomes, and it is the only rule a distribution must obey. The continuous version swaps the sum for an integral, which is why a density can exceed 1 at a point. Why this part exists: without normalisation two models cannot be compared — 0.6 means nothing until you know what the rest of the list adds up to.

Part 2 — Expectation is a weighted average

Expectation is E[X] = Σ x·p(x), the value you would average over many draws. A fair die gives (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5, a number no face ever shows. Over a dataset the same formula with p = 1/N is the mean loss per example. Why this part exists: training needs one number to minimise, and expectation is the honest way to collapse a distribution into one.

Part 3 — Variance is the spread around that average

Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²]. For the die, deviations from 3.5 are −2.5, −1.5, −0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5; their squares average 17.5/6 = 2.92, so the standard deviation is √2.92 ≈ 1.71. Two models can share a mean loss and differ here: one uniformly mediocre, one superb on most rows and catastrophic on a few. Why this part exists: a mean hides risk, so an average cannot tell you whether it came from consistency or from compensating extremes.

Part 4 — Likelihood flips which thing is fixed

p(data | θ) fixes the parameters and asks about the data. Training fixes the data — you already have it — and varies the parameters; that reversed view is the likelihood L(θ). Three heads in three tosses gives L(0.7) = 0.7³ = 0.343 and L(0.5) = 0.125, with only the hypothesis changing between them. Why this part exists: the forward question is about data you have not collected, so it cannot be evaluated after the fact.

Part 5 — Independence multiplies, so logs add

Independent observations multiply: three heads is 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7. A long sequence gives thousands of factors below 1, and the product underflows to 0.0 near 10⁻³⁰⁸ in float64. Logs rescue it because log(ab) = log a + log b, turning 0.343 into 3 × (−0.357) = −1.070; the maximiser does not move, since log is monotonic. Why this part exists: once the product underflows, the loss becomes −log 0 = inf, and infinity has no gradient to descend.

Part 6 — Softmax turns scores into probabilities

Logits are unbounded scores, one per class. Exponentiate each and divide by the total: z = [2, 1, 0] becomes 7.389, 2.718, 1.000, summing to 11.107, so the probabilities are 0.665, 0.245, 0.090. The exponential does two jobs — it forces positivity and amplifies gaps, so one logit of lead becomes a probability ratio of e ≈ 2.72. Why this part exists: raw scores break the sum-to-1 rule, and a loss built on them would not be a likelihood at all.

Part 7 — Training minimises the negative log

Maximising L and minimising −log L search for the same parameter value, and the second is numerically sane. The objective NLL(θ) = −Σ log p_θ(yᵢ | xᵢ) is a sum of positive terms, differentiated one term at a time. On a softmax output that expression is cross-entropy exactly, with nothing left over: a likelihood of 0.343 becomes a loss of 1.07. Why this part exists: every optimizer minimises, so without the minus sign the entire stack would walk away from the answer.

What to notice. The likelihood keeps climbing toward p = 1.0: three heads and no tails make "always heads" the maximum-likelihood answer, which is absurd. Three observations cannot separate 0.9 from 1.0. The objective is doing its job — shrinking surprise — and it will happily accept an overconfident model when the data are thin. That gap is what a prior or more tosses fills.

If the truth is class 1 you pay 0.41; if it is class 3 you pay 2.41, since 0.090 was a confident dismissal. Confidence is rewarded only when it is correct, and that asymmetry is what the gradient reads.

Undo the log and the average probability given to the truth is e^−1.034 ≈ 0.356 — not the 0.5 you get by averaging 0.9, 0.5 and 0.1. Averages of probabilities and averages of log-probabilities answer different questions.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"Likelihood is the probability that my model is correct." It feels right because both quantities are numbers between 0 and 1 attached to a model, "maximum likelihood" sounds like "most likely model", and the fitted value does end up high.

Correction: L(θ) = P(data | θ), with the data fixed. Turning that around to P(θ | data) needs a prior over models and a Bayes step that training never takes. Likelihood ranks hypotheses about parameters for one dataset; it is not a score you can quote as "70% chance this model is right".

The tell: someone says "the model is 70% likely" when they mean the true class averaged probability 0.7. The second tell is a metrics table where total NLL improves purely because the evaluation set grew — sums scale with row count, means do not.

Retrieval check

  1. A model says p(heads) = 0.5. Two tosses land heads. What is the NLL?
    Answer

    L = 0.25, so NLL = −ln 0.25 = 1.386. Two independent observations multiply; the log turns that into two additions of 0.693.

  2. Why do we take the log of a likelihood before optimising it?
    Answer

    Products of many probabilities underflow to zero in floating point, and derivatives of products are messy. Logs turn products into sums and preserve the location of the maximum.

  3. Model A scores NLL 0.5 on the test set; model B scores 1.2. Which do you ship, and what does 1.2 mean in plain language?
    Answer

    Ship A. On average its predicted probability for the true class is e^−0.5 ≈ 0.61; B's is e^−1.2 ≈ 0.30. B is roughly twice as surprised by reality.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
for p in (0.5, 0.7):
    L = p ** 3
    print(f"p={p}: likelihood={L:.3f}  NLL={-np.log(L):.3f}")
z = np.array([2., 1., 0.])
probs = np.exp(z) / np.exp(z).sum()
print("softmax:", probs.round(3))
