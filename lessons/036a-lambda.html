Part of Topic 36 · Eigenvalues, eigenvectors & SVD — the directions that survive a matrix

Equation lab · the stretch factor

λ (lambda) — the stretch a direction allows

You can say what λ measures, spot an eigenvector from the arithmetic, and explain why an ordinary vector needs a second number.

Where this sits

A v = λ v

Lab 1 of 1 · only λ is lit; the matrix A and the vector v stay dim

Multiplying by a matrix turns almost every arrow it is handed. A few arrows come back pointing the same way, only longer or shorter. Those are the eigenvectors, and λ (lambda) is the factor by which they change. The matrix A = [[3,1],[1,3]] has exactly two of them.

The curve behind the equation

Take v = (1,0). The matrix sends it to Av = (3,1). No number λ can turn (3,1) into a multiple of (1,0), because every multiple of (1,0) has a zero second entry and this one has 1. So Av = λv fails for this v, and that failure is what the lab is about.

The best you can do is pick λ so that λv lands as close to Av as possible. The figure plots the squared miss between them for every candidate λ.

The two sliders describe one direction. The first moves the bottom, which is the λ that fits best. The second lifts the whole bowl, which is the part of Av that no multiple of v can reach. Eigenvector means that part is zero.

What to notice

The first two rows are eigenvectors: Av is a multiple of v and the miss is exactly zero. The last two are ordinary vectors. Each still has a best λ, the bottom of its own bowl, but that bottom sits above the axis, so no λ makes the equation exact.

λ is not a length. For v = (1,0) the answer Av has length √10 = 3.16, while the best λ is 3. λ is the factor you would apply to v, not the size of the result. Because A is symmetric, its two eigenvalues are real, its eigenvectors are perpendicular, and the misses for every other direction are positive.

The trap— λ

Most common wrong mental model

"Every vector has an eigenvalue." Every vector has a best-fit λ, and the bowl always has a bottom. An eigenvalue is stricter: it is a λ for which some non-zero v satisfies Av = λv with no miss at all.

Correction: eigenvalues belong to the matrix, not to the arrow you happen to test. This matrix has two, 4 and 2. A tested vector may sit on neither.

The tell: compute Av and λv separately and compare entries. If the two entries of Av are not the same multiple of the entries of v, you have a best fit, not an eigenpair.

Retrieval check— λ

  1. For the same matrix, test v = (1,−1). What is Av, and what is λ?
    Answer

    Av = (3−1, 1−3) = (2,−2) = 2v, so λ = 2. The two entries are both twice the entries of v, so the equation holds exactly and the miss is zero.

  2. Why is λ = 3 not an eigenvalue of A, even though 3 is the best stretch for (1,0)?
    Answer

    Because 3v = (3,3) while Av = (3,1). They differ in the second entry, so the miss is 1, not 0. The only exact stretches are those of (1,1) and (1,−1).
