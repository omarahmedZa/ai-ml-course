Equation lab · summing surprise

Σ — summing surprise over the data

You can total the per-row surprises, then divide by the row count so two datasets can be compared.

Where this sits

NLL(θ) = Σi −log pθ(yi | xi)

Lab 2 of 2 · only the sum is lit; the per-row term is dim

Σi (sigma) means "add this up over every row i". Each row contributes one number, −log p, the surprise of the answer the model gave little probability to. NLL is the total bill.

Three rows, one bill

Give the model three rows and let it put p = 0.5 on the true answer each time. Each row costs 0.693, so the total is 3 × 0.693 = 2.08. Adding is the whole content of Σ.

The bars below are that total for three identical rows at three levels of confidence. Same model, same three rows; only the probability changes.

Doubling the confidence roughly halves the bill here, but not exactly — the price is logarithmic, not proportional. That bend is why one mediocre row can outweigh several good ones: a single row at p = 0.05 costs 2.996, more than twenty rows at p = 0.9.

Sum then divide

A total grows with the number of rows, so it cannot compare two test sets of different size. Six good rows total 0.63; three mediocre ones total 1.16. The smaller bill belongs to the better model here, but only because the row counts are close.

Divide by n, the number of rows, and the number becomes per-row. The objective writes that as (1/n) Σi. The minimiser does not move: the same θ wins before and after the division.

Check the last two rows

Row 2: 0.105 + 0.357 + 0.693 = 1.155, and 1.155 / 3 = 0.385. Row 3: 3 × 0.105 = 0.315, and 0.315 / 3 = 0.105 — exactly one row's surprise, as it must be when every row is the same.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"A lower total NLL means a better model." Only on the same rows. Total is a sum, so a bigger test set always produces a bigger number, and a team can "improve" a score by evaluating on more data.

Correction: compare mean NLL — total divided by n — or keep the row count fixed. Report n next to the number.

The tell: double the test set, and the total doubles while the model is untouched. If a metric can be moved by changing how much data you looked at, it is a sum, not a score.

Retrieval check

  1. Three rows each get p = 0.9 on the truth. What is the total NLL, and the mean?
    Answer

    Each row costs 0.105, so the total is 0.32 and the mean is 0.105. The mean equals one row's surprise because all three rows are alike.

  2. Model A totals 4.0 over 1,000 rows. Model B totals 2.0 over 100 rows. Which has the lower mean?
    Answer

    A: 4.0 / 1,000 = 0.004 per row against B's 2.0 / 100 = 0.02. A's total is twice as large and its loss is five times smaller. Totals across different row counts say nothing.
