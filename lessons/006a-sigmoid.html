Equation lab · linear and logistic regression

σ(z) — from score to probability

You can turn a weighted sum into a probability and find where the model switches sides.

Where this sits

p = σ(z) , z = w·x + b

The logistic model · one weighted sum, pushed through one squashing function

σ (sigma) is the sigmoid: σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z). It takes any number z and returns a value between 0 and 1. p is the predicted probability of the positive class; w is the weight, b the bias, and x the input.

The dot product w·x adds up each feature times its weight; b shifts the total. That total is z, and it can be any real number — large, small, or negative. σ is the only step that makes it look like a probability.

z is a log-odds, not a score

Odds compare the chance of yes with the chance of no: p / (1 − p). The log-odds is the logarithm of that ratio, and z is exactly a log-odds. So z = 0 means odds of 1:1, which gives p = 0.5.

Positive z favours yes; negative z favours no. z = 2 means odds of e² ≈ 7.39 to 1 (e ≈ 2.72), so p = 7.39 / 8.39 ≈ 0.88. The output is a probability; the input is not.

Where the boundary sits

The decision boundary is the set of x where p = 0.5. That happens when z = 0, so the boundary solves w·x + b = 0. For one input with w = 2 and b = 1: 2x + 1 = 0, so x = −0.5.

On one side of −0.5 the model says yes; on the other it says no. σ changes the confidence, not the location. It is monotone, so it never moves which side wins.

Why not a straight line

A line fitted to 0/1 labels can predict 1.4 or −0.3, which are not probabilities. σ restricts the range to (0, 1) and makes the output readable as a rate. It also keeps the log-loss smooth, which is why training behaves well.

Check the last row

Odds 7.39 means 7.39 yes for every 1 no, so p = 7.39 / (7.39 + 1) = 7.39 / 8.39 ≈ 0.88.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

“z = 2 means twice the probability of z = 1.” No. Adding 1 to z multiplies the odds by e ≈ 2.72; it adds a fixed amount to the log-odds. Probability moves by different amounts depending on where you start.

Correction: read z on the odds scale and p on the probability scale. z = 2 is odds 7.4:1, p = 0.88; z = 4 is odds 54.6:1, p = 0.98.

The tell: raise z by 1 from 0 and p rises by 0.23. Raise it by 1 from 2 and p rises by only 0.07. Equal steps in z give unequal steps in p — that is the squash at work.

Retrieval check

  1. What is σ(0), and why that value?
    Answer

    0.5. z = 0 is log-odds zero, which means odds 1:1 — an even chance, so the probability is one half.

  2. With w = 2 and b = 1, where is the decision boundary?
    Answer

    At x = −0.5, from 2x + 1 = 0. There p = 0.5 and the model is indifferent; the boundary is a point in one dimension.
