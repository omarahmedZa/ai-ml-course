Trees & ensembles

You can explain axis-aligned splits, bagging vs boosting, and when trees beat linear models.

Why this matters

On tabular data — spreadsheets, medical records, sensor tables — gradient-boosted trees are still the strongest default. Your project may well beat a neural network with 200 lines of XGBoost, and the exam will ask why.

The one core idea

A tree partitions feature space with axis-aligned cuts, choosing each cut that most reduces impurity. An ensemble is many trees combined — bagging averages away variance, boosting builds a sequence that removes bias.

How it actually works

  • Split selection is greedy: try every feature and threshold, keep the cut with the largest impurity drop. No global optimisation — a known weakness.
  • Impurity: Gini 1 − Σ pₖ² or entropy −Σ pₖ log pₖ for classification; variance/MSE for regression.
  • Stopping: max depth, min samples per leaf, or validation-based pruning. Unconstrained trees memorise.
  • Bagging (random forest): train many deep trees on bootstrap samples, average. Each tree has low bias, high variance; averaging cuts variance.
  • Boosting (GBM, XGBoost, LightGBM): train shallow trees sequentially, each fitting the previous ensemble's residual. Low bias grows slowly; variance is controlled by shrinkage and depth.

Worked example

1. Which split is better?

100 samples, 50 positive / 50 negative. Parent Gini = 1 − (0.5² + 0.5²) = 0.5.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"Trees can learn any pattern, including trends." They cannot extrapolate. A tree predicts the average of the training targets in a leaf, so it can never output a value outside the range it has seen. Ask it to forecast next year's growth and it flatlines.

Second trap: reading built-in feature importance as truth. Impurity-based importance is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features. Use permutation importance on held-out data instead (topic 31).

Retrieval check

  1. A node has 40 samples, 30 positive and 10 negative. Compute its Gini impurity.
    Answer

    1 − (0.75² + 0.25²) = 1 − (0.5625 + 0.0625) = 0.375. Pure nodes score 0; a 50/50 node scores 0.5, the maximum for two classes.

  2. You have 500 rows and a noisy target. Do you reach for a random forest or a boosted ensemble first, and why?
    Answer

    Random forest. With 500 noisy rows, boosting's sequential fitting will chase noise unless heavily regularised. Bagging is the safer default at small n.

  3. Why can a tree not predict a house price above every price in its training set?
    Answer

    Its prediction is a leaf average of training targets. No average can exceed the maximum observation, so the model is structurally incapable of extrapolation.
