Estimation & bias–variance

You can split an estimator's expected error into bias squared plus variance, and choose between two estimators on that total.

FoundationsProbability & Statistics

Why this matters— the two errors

An estimator is a recipe: feed it a sample, get a number. The sample mean is a recipe for a centre; a fitted polynomial is a recipe for a curve. Recipes differ in two ways — some aim at the wrong target, and some wobble from sample to sample. Knowing which is which tells you whether more data will help, whether a simpler model will help, and why the model with the best training score is often not the one to ship.

The failure it prevents. A team sees training error fall from 4.0 to 0.01 as they increase polynomial degree, and concludes that degree 12 is best. On held-out data it scores 9.5. Nothing was wrong with the fitting code; the recipe had become so sensitive to the particular sample that it memorised noise. Bias–variance is the accounting that predicts this before the test set does.

The one core idea— the two errors

Pick a target θ(theta) (theta) — a true mean, a true curve, a true probability. An estimator θ̂ (theta-hat) is a random quantity, because it is computed from a random sample. Its expected error has three parts:

E[(θ̂ − θ)²] = bias² + variance + noise

Bias is how far the average estimate sits from the truth: bias = E[θ̂] − θ. Variance is how far estimates scatter around their own average: E[(θ̂ − E[θ̂])²]. Noise is the irreducible part, the randomness in the data that no estimator can remove.

The first two are a trade-off. A rigid recipe has low variance and high bias; a flexible one has low bias and high variance. Total error is their sum, so the best model is usually neither the simplest nor the most flexible, but the one at the bottom of the U.

Error = bias² + variance + noise

Bias is aiming wrong. Variance is wobbling. More data shrinks variance, not bias.

How it actually works— the two errors

Part 1 — Bias is a systematic miss

Sample the heights of 100 people and report the average. That estimator is unbiased: repeat the study many times and the average of your averages equals the true mean. Now report max(height) instead. It is always below the true population maximum, so its average is below too — a bias that more data cannot fix, because the largest value in a larger sample only creeps upward.

Why this matters: bias points somewhere. If labels are noisy, predictions are pulled toward the middle, and extra rows do not remove that pull.

Part 2 — Variance is a wobble

Fit the same model to two samples of 100 and the coefficients differ. Variance measures how much. A degree-12 polynomial through 20 points swings wildly when one point moves; a straight line barely moves. High variance is a recipe that reads the sample too literally.

Why this matters: variance falls as 1/n, so adding rows or averaging models reduces it.

Part 3 — Regularisation buys bias to cut variance

Adding a penalty λ‖w‖²(lambda) (lambda) shrinks weights toward zero. The model can no longer fit every wrinkle, so bias rises, but the fitted curve stops chasing individual points, so variance falls. Ridge regression is exactly this trade, controlled by one dial.

Why this matters: it is the practical lever. When test error climbs while training error falls, increase the penalty.

Part 4 — Estimate the split in practice

Train many models on resampled datasets at one input point. The gap between their average and the truth estimates bias²; their spread estimates variance.

Why this matters: it turns a vague complaint about overfitting into two quantities, and says which dominates.

Worked example— the two errors

1. A biased estimator that wins

True mean μ = 10(mu) (mu), population spread σ = 2(sigma), sample size n = 4. The sample mean has standard error σ/√n = 1(square root), so its variance is 1 and its bias is 0. Total MSE: 0 + 1 = 1.00.

Now consider the shrunken estimator θ̂ = 0.95x̄ + 0.2. Its average is 0.95(10) + 0.2 = 9.7, so bias is −0.3 and bias² is 0.09. Its variance is 0.95² × 1 = 0.9025. Total: 0.09 + 0.9025 = 0.9925.

Read it as a sentence. Pulling the estimate 5% toward 9.7 costs 0.09 in squared bias and saves 0.0975 in variance, so total error falls slightly. Pulling harder costs 1.00 to save 0.36, and loses badly. The optimum is a small amount of bias.

2. The same U in model degree

Fit polynomials to 20 noisy points from a smooth curve. Training error falls monotonically with degree: 4.0 at degree 1, 1.1 at degree 3, 0.01 at degree 12. Test error does not: 4.2, 1.3, then 9.5.

What to notice. Degree 1 cannot bend, so both errors are high — bias. Degree 12 bends through every point, so a new sample gives a different curve — variance. Degree 3 sits near the bottom of the U, and only the test set reveals this.

3. More data moves the whole curve

With 200 points instead of 20, the variance of the degree-12 fit falls to roughly 20/200 = 0.1 of its former size, and test error may drop from 9.5 to 2.0. Overfitting is a property of a model at a sample size.

The trap— the two errors

Most common wrong mental model

"An unbiased estimator is always better." Unbiasedness is one virtue, not the only one. MSE cares about the sum, and a small bias can buy a large reduction in variance. The table above has an unbiased estimator losing to a biased one.

It feels right because bias sounds like a defect and zero sounds like a target. But the unbiased recipe can be so noisy that any single estimate is far off, while a slightly shrunken one is close on average. Shrinkage is the basis of ridge regression, James–Stein estimation and the empirical-Bayes methods behind modern hierarchical models.

Correction: compare estimators by expected squared error, not by bias alone. Report both terms, and say which one your sample size makes dominant.

The tell: training error falls while test error rises — variance is winning. Both errors stay high and adding data barely helps — bias is winning. The first calls for more data or regularisation; the second calls for a richer model or better features.

Retrieval check— the two errors

  1. An estimator has bias 0.4, variance 0.25 and the data noise is 0.1. What is its MSE?
    Answer

    0.4² + 0.25 + 0.1 = 0.16 + 0.25 + 0.1 = 0.51. Squaring the bias is the step people skip.

  2. Training error 0.02, test error 4.8, and the gap grows with model size. Which term dominates, and what are two fixes?
    Answer

    Variance dominates. Fixes: more training data (variance falls as 1/n), or restrict the model — stronger regularisation, fewer features, a simpler class.

  3. Why can adding data never remove bias?
    Answer

    Bias is a property of the estimator's target, not of sample size. Averaging more draws narrows the distribution around E[θ̂], but if that centre is not θ, the gap stays.

Build it in Colab— the two errors

Runnable, NumPy-only: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. It simulates many datasets, plots bias², variance and total error, and finds the degree at the bottom of the U.

import numpy as np
mu,sigma,n = 10.0,2.0,4
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
draws = rng.normal(mu,sigma,size=(20000,n)).mean(axis=1)
print(draws.mean())                  # about 10,bias near 0
print(draws.var())                   # about 1.0
shrunk = 0.95 * draws + 0.2
print(((shrunk - mu) ** 2).mean())   # about 0.99
