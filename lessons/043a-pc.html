Equation lab · the first principal axis

PC1 (the first component) — the axis that keeps the most spread

You can say what PC1 maximises, read a projected variance off a rotation, and turn it into an explained-variance ratio.

Where this sits

PC1 = the axis a that maximises ‖Xa‖² , with ‖a‖ = 1

Lab 1 of 1 · PC1 is lit; the cloud and its coordinates stay dim

PCA looks for the direction along which the data is most spread out. Project every point onto a candidate axis and measure the variance of those projections; the axis that makes it largest is the first principal component. A second axis, perpendicular to the first, takes the most of what is left.

Turning the axis

Take a simple cloud with variance 4 along one direction and variance 1 across it, uncorrelated, so the total is 5. An axis at angle θ from PC1 captures

captured = 1 + 3 cos²θ

At θ = 0 it captures all 4 of PC1. At 90° it captures the 1 that PC2 carries. In between, the captured share follows the square of the cosine, which is why a small turn costs very little.

The horizontal axis is cos²θ, not θ, because that is the quantity the variance depends on. Alignment 1 means your axis points straight down PC1; alignment 0 means it is perpendicular, along PC2. The marker is your current axis, and its height is the variance you keep.

The ratio is the captured variance divided by the total, 4 + 1 = 5. PC1 explains 80%, and that is the most any single axis can do here. The remaining 20% is the variance across the cloud, and only a second axis can reach it.

Notice the shape of the loss. The figure is drawn against cos²θ, so it is a straight line; read against the angle instead and it is a smooth step, almost flat near 0° and near 90° and steepest around 45°. A few degrees off PC1 cost almost nothing, which is why the leading component is stable to estimate from a noisy sample while the trailing ones are not.

Drag the two variance sliders to change the cloud. Widen the gap between PC1 and PC2 and the line steepens: rotation matters more. Set them equal and the line goes flat — every direction captures the same variance, and PC1 stops being special.

The trap— PC1

Most common wrong mental model

"PC1 is the feature with the biggest values." PC1 is a direction, usually a mix of features, chosen because projections onto it spread out most. A feature can have large raw numbers and still contribute little once the others are accounted for.

Correction: PC1 is defined by variance captured, not by the size of any column. Scaling the features changes the answer, which is why standardising first is the default.

The tell: if PC1 explains 100% of a two-feature cloud, one feature is an exact multiple of the other or the cloud is a straight line. Two genuine directions cannot both be captured by one axis.

Retrieval check— PC1

  1. A cloud has variance 4 along one direction and 1 across it. What share does PC1 explain?
    Answer

    The total is 5, and PC1 captures 4, so the ratio is 4/5 = 80%. The other 20% needs a second axis.

  2. An axis sits 45° from PC1. How much variance does it capture, and what is the ratio?
    Answer

    cos²45° = 0.5, so captured = 1 + 3(0.5) = 2.5 out of 5, a ratio of 50%. The capture is linear in cos²θ, so half the alignment gives half the variance here, not 80%.

Next, keep r components instead of one and the same arithmetic gives the total variance retained — the number that sets how many dimensions you can afford to drop.
