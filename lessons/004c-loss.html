Lab 3 of 9 · the whole expression is dimmed; only the loss is lit

A prediction is a number. A loss turns that number into a price: one number saying how bad the prediction was. Every later symbol in the objective is just bookkeeping over these prices.

One prediction, one price

The truth is 10. The model says 7. The gap is 3, and the price is 3² = 9. Slide the prediction and watch both the gap and the price move.

Why not absolute error

Absolute error is a legitimate loss, and it is more robust to outliers. Squaring is chosen for three reasons — each one matters later in the course.

  • Signs cancel. Without squaring, a +3 miss and a −3 miss add to 0 when summed (lab 5). The model would look perfect while being wrong in both directions.
  • Large misses dominate. A miss twice as large costs four times as much, which pushes the model to fix its worst predictions first.
  • Smooth everywhere. The derivative is 2 × residual; absolute error has a kink at 0, which breaks the gradient steps from topic 2.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"A low loss means the model is right." Loss is a price, not a verdict. It is unbounded — one wildly wrong prediction can outweigh a hundred good ones — and it depends on the units you measured in.

Correction: report loss and accuracy separately. Loss tells you how badly wrong; accuracy tells you how often.

The tell: convert your targets from metres to centimetres and the loss grows 10,000× while the model predicts exactly the same thing. If a metric can do that, it is a scale-sensitive price, not a correctness measure.

Retrieval check

  1. Truth 10, prediction 4. What is the squared loss?
    Answer

    Residual 6, loss 36. The gap is 6 but the price is 36 — that is the square at work.

  2. Why does one outlier dominate the average loss?
    Answer

    Because the price grows with the square. A residual of 10 contributes 100, which is more than a hundred residuals of 1. The mean is pulled toward the worst example.
