Offline RL & imitation

You can explain why a max over unseen actions breaks offline Q-learning, and compute the cost of compounding imitation error.

Reinforcement LearningRL

Why this matters— the constraint

Sometimes the agent cannot try things: a hospital has treatment logs but cannot run exploratory trials, and a driving stack has demonstrations but cannot crash on purpose. Offline RL learns a policy from a fixed dataset with no new interaction; imitation copies the logged actions and ignores rewards.

The failure it prevents. A policy trained by behaviour cloning gets 90% of actions right on held-out frames. Deployed, it swerves off the road in eight seconds. Each mistake puts the car in a state the data never covered, where the next is more likely — and 90% per step compounds to 0.9¹⁰ = 0.35 over ten steps.

The one core idea— the support

The dataset D is a set of transitions (s, a, r, s′) collected by some behaviour policy μ(mu). It covers a region of state–action space — the support. Inside that region you have evidence; outside it you have only a network's guess, and a Q-network asked about an action it never saw will return a number anyway.

In one line: only trust values for actions the data supports, and be pessimistic where it does not. Behaviour cloning stays inside the support by never scoring actions. Offline Q-learning must be constrained or penalised, because its target contains a max that selects unsupported actions with invented values.

target = r + γ maxₐ′ Q(s′, a′)

The max is the danger: if a′ is absent from D, its Q-value is extrapolation, and the policy will chase it.

How it actually works— the loop

Five parts, from a log file to a policy you can defend.

Part 1 — Treat the dataset as the environment

Each row is (s, a, r, s′). Record the behaviour policy and how often each pair appears. Why this part exists: coverage is the only guarantee you have; a rare action has a Q-value from a handful of rows, and a missing action has none.

Part 2 — Start with behaviour cloning

Train π_θ(a | s)(theta, pi) with cross-entropy against logged actions, exactly like classification: no reward, no bootstrapping. Why this part exists: it is stable and cheap, and often enough when the data is expert and the task forgiving.

Part 3 — Add rewards with offline Q-learning

Fit Q(s, a) to the TD target using only rows in D. The backup is unchanged; what changed is that the next state's best action is chosen from imagination rather than tried. Why this part exists: rewards let the policy improve past the behaviour policy.

Part 4 — Constrain or penalise the unseen

Three families: restrict the policy to actions near the data, penalise high Q-values on unseen actions (CQL), or avoid querying them by regressing toward in-data actions (IQL). Why this part exists: without one, the max is an exploit waiting to happen.

Part 5 — Evaluate without interacting

Use a held-out split of D, and report per-step error alongside a closed-loop estimate: importance-weighted returns, a learned simulator, or a guarded live test. Why this part exists: accuracy does not measure compounding drift.

Worked example— the arithmetic

1. Discounted returns, backwards

What to notice. The first step pays nothing yet is worth 1.71, more than any single reward. Running forwards gives 1.0, 1.9, 2.71 — rising the wrong way.

2. Compounding imitation error

Behaviour cloning is scored per step. If the policy is right 80% of the time and one mistake ends the episode, ten independent steps succeed with probability 0.8¹⁰.

What to notice. A 15-point gain per step — 80% to 95% — raises ten-step success from 11% to 60%, a factor of 5.6. Compounding is not a detail of the metric; it is the metric. That is why imitation is paired with a correction that returns the policy to the data's states.

3. The invented Q-value

At state s, the data contains two actions with returns 2.0 and 2.2, so Q(s, a₁) = 2.0 and Q(s, a₂) = 2.2. The next state's target takes a max over every action the network can score. An action a₃ never appears in D, and the network outputs Q(s′, a₃) = 5.0 because nothing corrected it.

The target becomes r + 0.9(5.0) instead of r + 0.9(2.2) = 1.98 + r, so every update pushes Q(s, a) up and the greedy policy selects a₃. The loss on the data still looks fine while the policy leaves the support. A CQL penalty on a₃, or a constraint that ranks only seen actions, removes the incentive.

The trap— the drift

Most common wrong mental model

"Offline RL is supervised learning with extra columns." The extra columns are easy; the difficulty is that the policy changes which states come next, and they may not be in the data.

It feels right because the loop really is regression on fixed rows, and the loss falls. But a supervised model is judged on its training distribution; a policy is judged on the distribution it creates. One bad action shifts the input, and the next prediction is off-distribution.

Correction: judge the policy by estimated return under its own state distribution, not by accuracy on logged rows. If the estimate relies on Q-values for actions the data never contains, it is extrapolation dressed as evaluation.

The tell: Q-values for rare actions climb while TD loss on the data falls — the curves diverge because the max is fed by imagination. A second tell is a policy choosing actions present in under 1% of rows. A third is 90% held-out accuracy with poor closed-loop success.

Retrieval check— recall

  1. Why is the max in the offline TD target dangerous?
    Answer

    It can select an action absent from the data, whose Q-value is an unconstrained guess. Training reinforces it and the policy seeks it out.

  2. When is behaviour cloning enough?
    Answer

    When the data is competent, small errors are tolerable, and the policy recovers inside the data's states. It cannot improve past the demonstrator.

  3. Per-step accuracy is 0.9 over 10 steps. What is the success probability, and what does it miss?
    Answer

    0.9¹⁰ ≈ 0.35 if steps are independent. Real drift is worse, because an error moves the agent to states where the next error is more likely.

Build it in Colab— the notebook

Runnable, NumPy-only: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. It computes returns backwards and makes the extrapolation error visible.

import numpy as np
rewards, gamma = [0., 1., 1.], 0.9
G, returns = 0., [0.] * len(rewards)
for t in reversed(range(len(rewards))):     # backwards, not forwards
    G = rewards[t] + gamma * G
    returns[t] = G
print(np.round(returns, 2))                 # [1.71 1.9 1.]
print(0.8 ** 10, 0.95 ** 10)                # 0.107 0.599
