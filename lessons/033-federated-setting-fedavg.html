Federated learning & FedAvg

You can describe the round protocol, write the averaging rule, and justify data locality.

Why this matters

Phones, hospitals and banks hold data that legally or practically cannot be centralised. Federated learning is the answer that keeps the data where it is and ships the model instead.

The one core idea

Move the model to the data, not the data to the model: each client trains locally, and the server averages the resulting weights.

How it actually works

  1. Broadcast: server sends current weights w_t to a sample of clients.
  2. Local training: each client runs E epochs of SGD on its own data, producing w_t^k.
  3. Upload: clients send weights (or updates) — never examples.
  4. Aggregate: server computes a sample-weighted average.
w_{t+1} = Σ_k (n_k / n) · w_t^k where n = Σ_k n_k
  • Why weight by nk: a client with 700 examples has a less noisy local model than one with 100. Equal weighting biases the global model toward small clients.
  • Rounds replace epochs: communication is the bottleneck, often by orders of magnitude. FedAvg exists to reduce round count, not local compute.
  • Data locality: raw data never leaves the device. Weights do — which matters for the privacy claims in topic 35.
  • Partial participation: only a fraction of clients are available per round (phones charge overnight, hospitals batch nightly).

Worked example

1. One aggregation step

Three clients with 100, 200 and 700 examples, one scalar parameter:

ClientnkLocal wWeightContribution
A1000.100.100.010
B2000.500.200.100
C7000.900.700.630
Global10000.740

Equal weighting would give (0.1+0.5+0.9)/3 = 0.50 — a model that ignores that 70% of the data says 0.9.

2. The cost model

100 rounds × 1,000 clients × 1 MB of weights = 100 GB of transfer, against a centralised training run that moves the dataset once. FedAvg's entire research programme is about cutting that number.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"FedAvg is equivalent to training on the pooled data." It is equivalent only when client distributions are identical and local training is a single step. With heterogeneous data, averaging weights averages different objectives, and the result can be worse than any client's own model.

Second trap: assuming the server sees updates, so privacy is solved. Gradients carry information about the data that produced them (topic 35).

Step by step

If the mechanism above went past you, walk it one click at a time. Each step is one sentence and one idea.

Retrieval check

  1. Two clients: 900 examples with local loss optimum 0.2, 100 examples with optimum 0.8. What does one FedAvg round produce?
    Answer

    0.9(0.2) + 0.1(0.8) = 0.26. The global model lands near the large client and largely ignores the small one — the source of fairness complaints in FL.

  2. Why is communication, not computation, the binding constraint?
    Answer

    Clients are often on mobile networks with limited upload bandwidth, and there may be millions of them. Local compute is comparatively cheap and parallel.

  3. What exactly does the server receive from a client?
    Answer

    Model weights or weight updates — never raw examples. That is the defining constraint, and also why privacy is not automatic.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
clients = [(100, 0.10), (200, 0.50), (700, 0.90)]
total = sum(n for n, _ in clients)
weighted = sum(n * w for n, w in clients) / total
equal = np.mean([w for _, w in clients])
print(f"weighted FedAvg: {weighted:.3f}  (equal weighting: {equal:.3f})")
