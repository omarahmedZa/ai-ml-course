Part of Topic 38 · Distributions & sampling — the spread of a bell

Equation lab · the spread parameter

σ (sigma) — the width of a bell

You can say what σ measures, read the 68/95 rule off it, and keep it apart from the standard error of the mean.

Where this sits

p(x) = 1/(σ√2π) · e−(x−μ)²/2σ²

Lab 1 of 1 · σ appears twice, in the height and in the exponent; μ only shifts the bell

A Gaussian is fixed by two numbers. μ (mu) says where its centre is; σ (sigma) says how far values typically sit from that centre. The height of the curve is not a separate choice: it is whatever makes the area under the curve equal 1, and that is why σ appears in front as well as in the exponent.

Wider bell, flatter curve

The figure shows the share of the data below a value, measured from the centre. Drag the steepness and watch the curve flatten or sharpen. A flat curve means values are spread far from μ; a sharp one means they crowd it.

The steepness is about 1.7/σ, so dragging it right makes σ smaller. Halve the steepness and you double the width: the same horizontal distance now carries less probability, and the curve takes longer to reach the top.

The rule is a statement about one observation, not about an average. A single reading from this bell lands within 2 points of 10 about 95 times in 100. Nothing in that statement improves when you collect more data; the bell itself does not narrow.

The average does narrow. If n independent readings have spread σ, their mean has spread σ/√n, called the standard error. With σ = 2: one reading has standard error 2, a mean of 100 readings has 0.2, and a mean of 400 has 0.1. Four times the data halves the error.

The trap— σ

Most common wrong mental model

"σ is the error of the mean." σ measures one observation's spread. The error of the mean is σ/√n, which is smaller and shrinks with sample size.

Correction: keep two names and two symbols. σ describes the population; σ/√n describes your estimate of its centre.

The tell: if the reported uncertainty falls when you add rows but the spread of the raw values does not, you are reading a standard error. If both stay the same, you are reading σ.

Retrieval check— σ

  1. A measurement has μ = 50 and σ = 5. Between which values do about 95% of single readings fall?
    Answer

    μ ± 2σ = 50 ± 10, so 40 to 60. About 95% of single readings land there; 68% land within one σ, from 45 to 55.

  2. σ = 4 and n = 64. What is the standard error of the mean, and what happens to it at n = 256?
    Answer

    At n = 64, 4/√64 = 4/8 = 0.5. At n = 256, 4/16 = 0.25. Four times the data halves the standard error, while σ stays 4.
