The objective, one symbol at a time · Lab 6

1/n — turn a total into a rate

You can explain why dividing by n makes two datasets comparable.

Where this sits

θ* = argminθ (1/n) Σi L( fθ(xi), yi )

Lab 6 of 9 · a total is divided by the number of examples that produced it

Read the inner part first. Σi means add up, and it runs over every example. n is how many examples there are. 1/n divides the total by that count, so the objective reports one number per example instead of one number per dataset.

A sum grows with the dataset

Hold one model fixed and score it on more rows. Suppose it is off by 1.5 points per example — the mean, or average price of a single row. The total is just that price times the number of rows.

Same model in all three rows. Nothing improved; nothing got worse. Yet the total moved by a factor of a hundred. A number that changes when you merely collect more data cannot tell you whether the model is any good.

Divide and the size disappears

The figure holds the mean at 1.5 and turns n from 10 up to 100. Watch which of the two columns moves.

The left bar is a total, and it climbs with every row you add. The right bar is a rate, and it does not budge. Dividing by n removes the size of the dataset and leaves the part you actually wanted: the price of a typical example.

Why it matters

Two models, each scored on its own dataset. The totals are in the third column and the rates in the fourth. Do not read the third column.

Which model is better? B, because 1.5 < 2.0 — its typical mistake is smaller. Now read the totals and you get the opposite verdict: 20 < 150, so A looks better. It is not. A was graded on a shorter exam and still missed by more per question.

Check both blanks

Row 1: 1.5 × 50 = 75. Row 2: 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. The totals differ by 15; the rates agree exactly.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"A smaller total means a better model." No. A total is a count-weighted quantity, so it is only comparable to another total from the same dataset, with the same n and the same units.

Correction: compare means. Compare totals only when n is identical on both sides.

The tell: if adding duplicate rows doubles your loss, you forgot the division. Duplicating a row raises n and the total together, so the mean — and the model — should not move at all.

Retrieval check

  1. A dataset has n = 20 and a total loss of 30. What is the mean?
    Answer

    30 ÷ 20 = 1.5. One and a half points per example.

  2. Dataset A has mean 2.0, dataset B has mean 1.5. Which is better, and why can't the totals decide?
    Answer

    B: 1.5 < 2.0. Totals scale with n, so a large sloppy dataset can out-total a small accurate one. Dividing puts both on the same per-example footing.
