RNN → LSTM: memory that survives

You can explain the fixed-vector bottleneck and how gates address it.

Why this matters

The LSTM is the last honest attempt to give a network memory without attention — and its failure is precisely the reason transformers exist. Exam questions love "why does the RNN forget?"

The one core idea

A vanilla RNN compresses everything seen so far into one fixed-size vector, updated every step. Gradients must travel back through every step, so long-range signal decays — gates give that signal a near-identity path.

How it actually works

h_t = tanh(W_h h_{t−1} + W_x x_t + b)
  • The hidden state h_t is the entire memory. Same size at step 1 and step 1,000 — that is the fixed-vector bottleneck.
  • BPTT (backprop through time) unrolls the sequence and multiplies a Jacobian per step. Repeated multiplication is the whole problem.
  • LSTM gates: f forget, i input, o output, each a sigmoid in [0,1].
    c_t = f ⊙ c_{t−1} + i ⊙ g — the cell state is updated additively.
  • Because f ≈ 1 is learnable, the cell state can carry information across many steps with gradient flowing undiminished. That is the trick: an additive, gated highway.
  • GRU is the cheaper cousin: two gates, no separate cell state.

Worked example

1. Decay per step

Suppose each step's Jacobian has magnitude 0.9 (a healthy value):

Steps back0.9ⁿ1.1ⁿ
50.591.61
200.126.73
500.005117

Even a well-behaved RNN has lost 99.5% of its gradient by 50 steps. Word 1 of a paragraph contributes nothing to the loss at word 60.

2. What the forget gate buys

With f = 0.99 held constant, after 100 steps the cell retains 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37 of the original signal — a 74× improvement over the 0.9 case above, and, importantly, learnable rather than architectural luck.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"LSTM solves long-term memory." It mitigates decay on synthetic tasks with clear signals. On real language, performance still degrades with distance, and the fixed-size state is still a bottleneck: 500 tokens must fit in 512 numbers.

Second trap: forgetting the sequence is sequential. Training cannot parallelise over time, which is why RNNs lost to transformers on long sequences — not because attention is more expressive per se.

Step by step

If the mechanism above went past you, walk it one click at a time. Each step is one sentence and one idea.

Retrieval check

  1. Why does a vanilla RNN struggle with a dependency 100 tokens apart?
    Answer

    The gradient passes through ~100 Jacobians; if their magnitudes average below 1, the signal decays exponentially. The state also has to hold all intermediate information in a fixed vector.

  2. What makes the LSTM cell state easier to train than a plain hidden state?
    Answer

    Its update is additive and gated: c_t = f·c_{t−1} + i·g. With f near 1, the gradient path is close to identity, so it does not get multiplied away each step.

  3. Name one problem gates do not fix.
    Answer

    The fixed-size state and sequential computation. Long inputs still must be compressed into one vector, and time steps still cannot be processed in parallel.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
# vanilla recurrence: h_t = tanh(W h_{t-1} + x_t)
W, x = np.array([[0.9]]), np.array([1.0])
h = np.zeros(1)
traj = []
for t in range(20):
    h = np.tanh(W @ h + x)
