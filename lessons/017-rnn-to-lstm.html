RNN → LSTM: memory that survives

You can explain the fixed-vector bottleneck and how gates address it.

Why this matters

An RNN is the smallest machine that turns a sequence of vectors into one vector. Every pre-2017 translation system used one, and small models on cheap hardware still do — time series, on-device keyword spotting, sensor logs. The LSTM is the version that made the idea survive contact with long inputs.

Consider a review classifier that scores 92% in testing and 61% in production. Training reviews were short; real ones run to 300 tokens, and the model's decision tracks the last clause only, because the gradient carrying the opening verdict never reached the weights. On an exam the same misunderstanding costs marks in a single sentence: "explain why a vanilla RNN forgets" is answerable in one line about repeated multiplication, and unanswerable if you believe the state simply runs out of room.

The one core idea

A reader holds one small notepad. After each word, the reader crosses out the whole page and rewrites it from the page plus the new word. The notepad is 512 numbers wide on word 1 and still 512 on word 1,000, so nothing is ever added to it — only overwritten. That single constraint explains both the memory limit and the training difficulty.

In one line: the past is carried by repeated multiplication, so a factor of 0.9 per step leaves 0.9¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.000027 after a hundred steps. Nothing vanished at any one step; the loss is compounding, which is why it is invisible in a print statement and obvious in a gradient norm.

How it actually works

Seven parts. The first four describe plain recurrence and why it fails; the last three describe the repair.

Part 1 — Fold the old state and the new token into one vector

At each step the model computes h_t = tanh(W_h h_{t−1} + W_x x_t + b). With 300-dimensional embeddings and 512 hidden units, W_x holds 300 × 512 = 153,600 weights and W_h holds 512 × 512 = 262,144. Why this part exists: without the W_h h_{t−1} term the model would be a feed-forward network looking at one token, with no sequence at all.

Part 2 — Reuse the same weights at every step

Step 1 and step 1,000 share one set of weights; that sharing is what lets a model trained on 40-token sentences accept 400-token ones. A separate weight matrix per position would need 1,000 × 416,256 weights for a 1,000-step sequence. Why this part exists: parameter sharing is the only reason sequence length is not a hyperparameter baked into the architecture.

Part 3 — Unroll the loop to train it

Backpropagation through time draws the 200 steps as 200 layers with tied weights, then applies the chain rule across all of them. The gradient reaching step 1 is a product of 200 Jacobians, one per hop. Why this part exists: there is no other way to credit an early token for a late error, so training must walk the whole chain.

Part 4 — Watch the product shrink

Each Jacobian has a typical magnitude, and for a plain RNN it usually sits below 1. At 0.9 per hop, 50 hops leave 0.005 of the signal and 100 hops leave 0.0000266. Why this part exists: once the factor is below 1 the product approaches zero, so early tokens stop influencing the loss and their weights stop learning.

Part 5 — Watch it explode instead

The identical arithmetic with a factor of 1.1 gives 117 after 50 hops and 13,780 after 100. The model is the same shape; only the typical size of the Jacobian differs. Why this part exists: exploding gradients produce inf and then NaN in one step, and clipping the gradient norm to 5 is the standard guard against it.

Part 6 — Add a cell state and gates that scale it

An LSTM keeps a second vector c and computes c_t = f ⊙ c_{t−1} + i ⊙ g, where f, i and o are sigmoids in (0, 1). Because f multiplies the old state directly, the gradient path is a product of forget gates rather than of full Jacobians. Why this part exists: a learnable f = 0.99 keeps most of the signal for a hundred steps, which no fixed architecture can promise.

Part 7 — Count what the GRU saves

A GRU drops the separate cell state and keeps two gates, so it needs three weight matrices where the LSTM needs four. At 300 inputs and 512 hidden that is 1,248,768 parameters against 1,665,024 — exactly 25% fewer. Why this part exists: on small datasets the smaller model often wins, and the saving is free at inference as well.

It also shows where the gate cost comes from: every gate is a full copy of the same two matrices, so a fourth gate is a 25% tax on the layer, paid once and then multiplied by depth and by every layer you stack.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"The LSTM solves long-term memory." It feels right because the paper's headline experiment — remember one bit for a hundred steps — works, and because the forgetting curve visibly flattens once gates are added.

The correction: gates remove the gradient obstacle on a clean synthetic task, not the capacity obstacle on real language. Five hundred tokens still have to fit in 512 numbers, performance still falls as the dependency lengthens, and the computation is still sequential. The state is a summary, and a summary of a page is lossy no matter how well it is trained.

The tell: hold the sequence length fixed and move the cue earlier. If accuracy is stable at 20 tokens of distance and near chance at 200 while total length never changes, you are watching the state lose information, not the model run out of context. A second tell is a training loop whose step time grows linearly with sequence length while the GPU sits mostly idle — that is the sequential dependency, and more hardware will not shorten it.

Retrieval check

  1. Why does a vanilla RNN struggle with a dependency 100 tokens apart?
    Answer

    The gradient passes through ~100 Jacobians; if their magnitudes average below 1, the signal decays exponentially. The state also has to hold all intermediate information in a fixed vector.

  2. What makes the LSTM cell state easier to train than a plain hidden state?
    Answer

    Its update is additive and gated: c_t = f·c_{t−1} + i·g. With f near 1, the gradient path is close to identity, so it does not get multiplied away each step.

  3. Name one problem gates do not fix.
    Answer

    The fixed-size state and sequential computation. Long inputs still must be compressed into one vector, and time steps still cannot be processed in parallel.
