The RL frame: states, actions, rewards

You can formalize a task as (S, A, P, R, γ) and compute a return.

Why this matters

Reinforcement learning trains by reward instead of by a labelled answer key. That is the only option when the right move depends on a long future: a robot arm stacking cups, a scheduler picking the next job, a game engine hunting a winning line. Nobody can label each decision as correct, because correctness only shows up in how the episode ends.

What you can write down is what you want to happen — points for the goal, a penalty for the crash — and this frame turns that wish into five fields an algorithm can consume. It also prevents an expensive failure: optimising the number you wrote instead of the outcome you meant. A warehouse robot rewarded +1 per shelf passed learns to circle one shelf forever; the code and the arithmetic are right, the behaviour is useless. In an exam, "25 cells, four moves, a 10% slip, −1 per step, +10 at the goal" is a complete specification worth marks.

The one core idea

Picture the agent as a traveller in a maze who is paid as it walks: at each junction it sees one room, picks a corridor, and collects whatever coins lie there. Good behaviour is not "take the nearest coin"; it is "take the route whose total coins, with a haircut for lateness, is largest". The haircut is the discount, because a coin in hand can be spent now.

In one line: return is a discounted running total of rewards, and the agent's only job is to make it large. With a reward of 1 arriving at three consecutive steps and γ = 0.5, the return is 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 1.75 — not 3.

How it actually works

Seven parts, one field each.

Part 1 — A state is what the agent can see

S is the set of situations the agent can be in; a 5×5 grid world has 25, one per cell. What counts as a state is a modelling choice: anything the agent cannot distinguish must be merged, anything needing a different move must be split. Why this part exists: if the state omits where the goal is, a cell beside the goal and the same cell in a copy of the maze look identical, and no policy is right in both.

Part 2 — Actions are the legal choices

A holds the moves, written A(s) when the legal set varies by state. The grid offers up, down, left, right — four choices, so 25 × 4 = 100 state–action pairs. Why this part exists: the action set fixes the size of everything downstream. Add a "wait" action and every value table grows by 25 entries; forget to allow a move and the optimal route does not exist in your model.

Part 3 — Transitions are the world's rules

P(s′|s,a) is the chance of landing in s′ after choosing a in s. It belongs to the environment, not the agent, and it is the field people skip. A 10% slip means the intended direction happens 90% of the time and the agent ends up somewhere it never asked to go. Why this part exists: without probabilities over next states, the average return has nothing to average over.

Part 4 — Reward is the only feedback

R(s,a,s′) is one number per transition. The grid pays −1 per step and +10 at the goal. That −1 does real work: a four-step route beats a nine-step route by five points before discounting. Why this part exists: reward is the entire specification of what you want. The other fields describe the world; this one describes your preference, and an agent satisfies it literally.

Part 5 — The discount prices time

γ sits between 0 and 1 and shrinks each reward by one more factor of itself per step of delay. The quantity 1/(1−γ) is the effective horizon: γ = 0.95 gives 20 steps, γ = 0.99 gives 100. Why this part exists: it encodes urgency, and it makes an endless stream of rewards add up to a finite number, so "this policy is better" can be true at all.

Part 6 — The policy is the deliverable

π maps each state to a choice: deterministic a = π(s), or stochastic π(a|s) giving a probability per action. Why this part exists: without a named policy there is nothing to deploy. A value table is an intermediate result; the artefact is a rule that says what to do.

Part 7 — Return is the objective

G_t = r_{t+1} + γr_{t+2} + γ²r_{t+3} + … is the whole discounted stream from time t onward. Why this part exists: reward and return are different quantities for a reason. An agent will accept a reward of −1 now for a larger return, because −1 is not what the update maximises — and most misreadings start by treating the two words as synonyms.

Worked example

1. Five rewards, one discount

A reward of 2 at each of five steps, γ = 0.5. Write every power of γ down; skipping them is how a factor-of-two error survives to the report.

What to notice. The last two rewards together add 0.375, less than a fifth of the first alone. A discount of 0.5 removed 6.125 of the nominal 10: not a mild correction, a re-ordering of strategies. It also explains why early mistakes matter more than late ones — in a short episode the first step outweighs the rest combined.

A reward of 11 arriving at step 100 against 1 available now, at γ = 0.9. The arithmetic is repeated multiplication.

Here the agent takes 1 now over 11 at step 100 by a factor of about 3,400 — the first thing to inspect when an agent refuses to invest in a late payoff.

3. What γ buys you in horizon

The effective horizon 1/(1−γ) is also the total value of an endless stream of unit rewards, because 1 + γ + γ² + … = 1/(1−γ).

γArithmeticEffective horizon
0.91 / 0.110 steps
0.991 / 0.01100 steps
0.9991 / 0.0011,000 steps

What to notice. Moving γ from 0.99 to 0.999 multiplies the horizon by ten while looking like a rounding change. The cost is variance: distant rewards that were crushed to nothing now count at nearly full value, so an estimate from a few sampled episodes gets much noisier. Long-horizon tasks are hard for this reason, not because the algebra is harder.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"The agent maximises reward." It feels right because reward is the only number the environment hands over, and in a one-step problem the two words mean the same thing.

Correction: the objective is return, so γ decides which policy wins. At γ = 0.9, a reward of 11 arriving after 100 steps is worth 0.0003 — an agent maximising return takes the 1 on offer now, every time. Change γ and the preferred behaviour changes without editing a single reward.

Second trap: writing a proxy for the goal instead of the goal. Told to maximise time alive, an agent learns to avoid finishing. Reward design is the specification.

The tell: if you can raise the agent's score without improving the task, you are measuring a proxy, and the score climbs while the demo gets worse. The other tell is a γ you never chose — libraries often default it to 0.99. Set it to 0 and rerun: if behaviour collapses to grabbing the next reward while the task needs planning, that value was carrying the design.

Retrieval check

  1. Reward 2 at every step, 5 steps, γ = 0.5. Compute the return.
    Answer

    2(1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.0625) = 3.875. Undiscounted it would be 10 — the discount more than halves it.

  2. What does γ = 0 do to the agent's behaviour?
    Answer

    It becomes entirely myopic: only r_{t+1} matters. Any action with a delayed payoff is invisible, which is usually a bug, not a design.

  3. Why is a continuing task problematic without a discount?
    Answer

    The return is an infinite sum of positive rewards — it diverges, so no policy can be "better". γ < 1 makes the sum finite and the comparison meaningful.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. Sections, plots, sliders and line-by-line notes — run the cells in order, then change one number and rerun.

import numpy as np
def discounted(rewards, gamma):
    return sum((gamma ** k) * r for k, r in enumerate(rewards))
for gamma in (0.0, 0.9, 0.99):
    print(f"gamma={gamma}: return over 10 unit rewards = {discounted([1]*10, gamma):.3f}")
    print(f"           a reward of 11 at step 100 is worth {11 * gamma**100:.6f}")
print("effective horizon 1/(1-gamma):", [round(1/(1-g), 1) for g in (0.9, 0.99, 0.999)])
