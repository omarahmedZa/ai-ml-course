Overfitting in deep learning

You can choose among dropout, weight decay, augmentation and early stopping for a given symptom.

Why this matters

Networks have enough parameters to memorise any dataset you can afford to label. The difference between a demo and a shipped model is usually one of these four levers, applied for the right reason.

The one core idea

Every regulariser works by making the model's job harder during training so it cannot rely on memorisable, brittle cues — and each one attacks a different kind of brittleness.

How it actually works

LeverMechanismUse when
DropoutZero each activation with probability p during training; rescale by 1/(1−p); identity at inferenceLarge fully-connected layers, small data
Weight decayAdd λ‖w‖² to the loss; pulls weights toward 0Weights growing large; always a sane default
AugmentationRandom crops, flips, colour jitter, mixup — label-preserving transformsImages, audio, text; the cheapest data you will ever get
Early stoppingKeep the checkpoint with best validation lossAlways. Costs nothing but bookkeeping

Dropout works because no unit can rely on a specific neighbour being present — it must be useful on its own. Weight decay works because large weights produce sharp, high-curvature functions that fit noise. Augmentation works because it teaches invariance directly instead of hoping the data implies it.

Worked example

1. A 28-point gap

Train accuracy 0.99, validation 0.71, 8,000 images.

InterventionTrainVal
Baseline0.990.71
+ dropout 0.3 on FC layers0.880.82
+ flip/crop augmentation0.860.91

Training accuracy fell and the model got better. That is the signature of a regulariser working: you are trading memorisation for generalisation.

2. Picking the lever

Symptom drives choice. Huge gap with plenty of data → augmentation first (it adds information). Huge gap with 500 rows → dropout and weight decay. Gap that appears only late in training → early stopping. No gap but bad performance → you are not overfitting; regularising will make it worse.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"Dropout improves the model." It does nothing at inference — it is identity. Its only effect is on the training dynamics. Leaving it on at evaluation produces random, noisy predictions and a mysteriously bad validation score.

Second trap: augmentation that changes the label. Horizontal flips of digits turn 6 into 9; vertical flips of text destroy meaning. Every transform must preserve what you are asking the model to predict.

Retrieval check

  1. Dropout with p = 0.4 is active. What happens to the surviving activations during training, and at inference?
    Answer

    Survivors are divided by 1 − 0.4 = 0.6, so the expected sum is unchanged. At inference dropout is off and no rescaling happens — that is why the two modes must never mix.

  2. Your train and validation loss are both 1.4 and flat. Should you add dropout?
    Answer

    No. Equal, high losses mean underfitting. Regularisation increases bias; you need capacity, better features, or more training.

  3. Why is augmentation usually the first lever for images?
    Answer

    It encodes real invariances (a cat is still a cat when shifted) and multiplies effective data without new labels. Dropout only limits capacity; augmentation adds information.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 20)
y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * x) + rng.normal(0, .25, 20)
