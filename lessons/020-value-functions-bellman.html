Value functions & the Bellman equation

You can write V and Q and explain the recursive expectation.

Why this matters

A value function is a price tag on a situation: one number answering "if I am here, and behave like this from now on, how much reward is left?" Every algorithm in this unit uses one. Value iteration ranks states with it, Q-learning stores one per state–action pair, actor–critic trains a critic to score the actor, and policy gradient subtracts a state value to steady its updates.

Two kinds of damage come from getting it wrong. In an exam it is a two-line backup — r = 1, γ = 0.9, V(s′) = 2, so Q = 2.8 — and candidates lose the marks by adding the reward twice or forgetting the discount. In a project it is silent inflation: bootstrap past a terminal state and every finished episode is credited with a future that does not exist. With γ = 0.9 and a phantom V = 5, each ending gains 4.5 of invented value, so the average return climbs, the training curve looks healthy, and the policy gets worse.

The one core idea

Treat a state's value as the price of a ticket entitling you to the rest of the trip. Today's price is today's reward plus the price of a ticket from wherever you land next. The far future never has to be enumerated, because it is already folded into the next state's price — the recursion does the bookkeeping.

In one line: V(s) = r + γV(s′), a number defined in terms of a smaller copy of itself. With r = 1, γ = 0.9 and V(s′) = 2, the value is 1 + 1.8 = 2.8.

How it actually works

Seven parts, from the quantity being predicted to the update that learns it from samples.

Part 1 — Expectation, because the future is random

V^π(s) is the expected return: a probability-weighted average over every future the policy — the rule that picks actions — might produce. With a 10% slip, a thousand episodes from s average the intended outcome and the slipped one in proportion to how often each occurs. Why this part exists: without an average, the value of one state would differ on every visit and no table could ever settle.

Part 2 — One number per state

The value table has |S| entries, so 25 for a 5×5 grid. Each entry is a prediction about the future, not a record of the past. Why this part exists: this is the compression that makes the problem tractable. A distribution over thousands of trajectories becomes a single scalar, and "which state is better" becomes a comparison of two numbers.

Part 3 — Q adds the first decision

Q^π(s,a) is the expected return after committing to action a in s, then following π. It has |S|×|A| entries: 25 × 4 = 100 for the grid. Why this part exists: V says a state is good but not which move made it good. Choosing requires comparing actions at the same state, and only Q is indexed by the action.

Part 4 — V is Q averaged over the policy

V^π(s) = Σ_a π(a|s) Q^π(s,a)

With π(left) = 0.7, π(right) = 0.3, Q(left) = 2.8 and Q(right) = 1.5: 0.7 × 2.8 + 0.3 × 1.5 = 2.41. Why this part exists: it links the two tables. Change the policy and V changes even when Q does not, which is how a new value table is computed without re-simulating anything.

Part 5 — The Bellman backup replaces an infinite sum

V^π(s) = Σ_a π(a|s) Σ_{s′} P(s′|s,a)[ R(s,a,s′) + γ V^π(s′) ]

Read it inside out: for each action, average over next states; for each next state, take its reward plus the discounted value there; then average over actions. Why this part exists: the definition of return is an endless sum, and this equation replaces it with one step plus a table lookup — a finite computation you can run, and running it repeatedly is policy evaluation.

Part 6 — The max turns evaluation into control

Replace the average over actions with a maximum and you get Q*(s,a) = Σ_{s′} P[R + γ max_{a′} Q*(s′,a′)]. The target now asks what the best next action would give, not what the current policy does. Why this part exists: evaluation scores the policy you already have, and the max scores the best one available. Without it you can rank policies but never improve one.

Part 7 — TD learning samples instead of averaging

In practice P is unknown, so take the one transition you observed and move partway toward it: V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)]. With V(s) = 2.0, r = 1, γ = 0.9, V(s′) = 2 and α = 0.1, the target is 2.8, the error is 0.8, and the new value is 2.08. Why this part exists: you cannot sum over next states you have never enumerated, and α = 0.1 is why one sample moves the estimate by 10% rather than all the way.

Worked example

1. One backup, two actions

Action a pays 1 and lands in s′ with V(s′) = 2; γ = 0.9.

StepArithmeticValue
Immediate reward11.0
Discounted future0.9 × 21.8
Q(s,a)1.0 + 1.82.8
Other actiongiven1.5
V*(s)max(2.8, 1.5)2.8

What to notice. One backup is one decision: the maximum picks the action and the state's value inherits it. The losing action is not bad, it is 1.3 behind — and if your estimates carry noise of ±1.5, that ranking can flip. Nothing in this table tells you how confident the estimates are, which is exactly the gap exploration fills in topic 21.

2. A policy that sometimes chooses wrong

Same state, but the policy mixes: π(left) = 0.7 with Q = 2.8, π(right) = 0.3 with Q = 1.5.

TermArithmeticContribution
Left branch0.7 × 2.81.96
Right branch0.3 × 1.50.45
V^π(s)1.96 + 0.452.41

What to notice. The gap 2.8 − 2.41 = 0.39 is the exact price of that 0.3 chance of taking the worse action. A deterministic policy would bank the full 2.8. This is also how to read a policy: a large gap means hesitating is expensive, and the fix is to sharpen π, not to touch Q.

3. A backup with two possible next states

One action from s pays 1 either way. With probability 0.8 it reaches s′ with V = 2; with probability 0.2 it reaches a pit with V = −1. γ = 0.9.

BranchReward + γVProbabilityContribution
To s′1 + 0.9 × 2 = 2.80.82.24
To pit1 + 0.9 × (−1) = 0.10.20.02
Total—1.02.26

What to notice. The reward happened to be 1 in both branches, so the probabilities sum to 1 and it survives intact at 1.0; only the future is averaged, giving 1 + 0.9 × (1.6 − 0.2) = 2.26. Make the pit pay 0 instead and the value falls to 2.24 − 0.18 = 2.06, because now the branch-specific reward is being averaged too.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"V and Q are the same thing." It feels right because both are called value, both are estimated numbers, and in the example above V* does equal the best Q.

Correction: shapes differ, and so do the questions. V has one entry per state; Q has one per state–action pair. You cannot pick an action from V alone without a model of the dynamics, which is why Q-learning learns Q and why its argmax needs no model.

Second trap: bootstrapping off a terminal state. There is no future after termination, so γV(s′) there adds phantom value and inflates every episode that ends.

The tell: if you catch yourself writing V[s] and then asking which action that was, you are holding the wrong table. The terminal version shows up in the numbers rather than the code: the value of the last state before the end must equal the final reward, with no γ term. If that entry is larger than the reward, something is being added after the episode is over.

Retrieval check

  1. r = 0, γ = 0.9, V(s′) = 5. What is Q(s,a)?
    Answer

    0 + 0.9 × 5 = 4.5. The whole value is deferred — which is exactly why discounting matters for long horizons.

  2. Why does the optimal Bellman equation contain a max while the policy-evaluation one contains a sum over actions?
    Answer

    Evaluation averages over the policy's action distribution. Optimisation asks what the best action would give, so it maximises instead of averaging.

  3. What is the TD error, and what does its sign tell you?
    Answer

    r + γV(s′) − V(s): the surprise between the bootstrapped target and the current estimate. Positive means the state was undervalued; negative, overvalued.
