Equation lab · surprise

−log p — the price of surprise

You can turn a probability into a loss and say why a confident miss costs so much more than a hesitant one.

Where this sits

NLL(θ) = Σi −log pθ(yi | xi)

Lab 1 of 2 · only the per-row term is lit; the sum over rows is dim

p is the probability the model gave to the answer that actually happened. Not the top guess — the true label. If the model said 0.9 and the label is right, p = 0.9; if it said 0.05, p = 0.05.

From probability to price

A probability is a number between 0 and 1. Its logarithm is therefore zero or negative, and the minus sign flips that into a price you can add up: −log p = 0 when the model was certain and right, and it grows as p shrinks.

The log is base e, so the unit is the nat. It does not matter for comparisons; only the shape of the curve does.

Notice the asymmetry. The curve is almost flat over the right half and nearly vertical on the left. Halving a good probability is affordable; halving a bad one is not.

Confident and wrong

A wrong answer is not one price. It is priced by the probability the model had assigned to the right one.

0.014.605certain, and wrong

From 0.9 to 0.01 the loss grows about 44 times. The model made one mistake in both rows; only its confidence changed. −log p punishes overconfidence, which is exactly what you want a forecaster scored on.

Read the table as a scoring rule, not a verdict: it rewards probability placed on the truth, and it charges nothing for probability placed anywhere else.

Check the last two rows

−log 0.25 = 1.386 and −log 0.05 = 2.996. Both rows are wrong; the second one is wrong and dismissive, so it pays about twice as much.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"Every wrong answer costs the same." It does not. Cross-entropy scores the quote, not the label. Two models can both be wrong on the same row and pay 0.7 and 4.6.

Correction: the loss asks how much probability you gave to the truth. A miss you saw coming is cheap; a miss you ruled out is expensive.

The tell: a model whose accuracy is flat while its loss climbs is not getting worse at answering — it is getting more confident and staying wrong. Look at the probabilities, not just the labels.

Retrieval check

  1. The true label gets p = 0.5 from the model. What is the row's loss, and why is it not zero?
    Answer

    −log 0.5 = 0.693. The model left half the probability elsewhere, so even the right answer was only a coin flip. Only p = 1 pays nothing.

  2. Which is worse: p = 0.4 on the truth, or p = 0.04?
    Answer

    p = 0.04, by a lot: 3.22 against 0.92. The loss is not linear in p, so a tenfold drop in probability adds roughly 2.3 to the bill.
