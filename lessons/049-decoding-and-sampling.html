Decoding & sampling

You can turn logits into a token with greedy, top-k, top-p and temperature, and say what each knob does.

Language & TransformersTokens & Decoding

Why this matters— decoding

A language model does not produce text. It produces one score per vocabulary item for the next position, and something must choose a token. That chooser is the decoder; its settings decide whether the model gives a stable extraction or a new paragraph each run. Temperature, top-k and top-p change accuracy, repetition and cost on the same weights.

The failure it prevents. An exam asks for the difference between top-k and top-p. "Both cut off unlikely tokens" earns half marks; the rest comes from saying top-k keeps a fixed count while top-p keeps the smallest set whose probability sums to p, so the count adapts to confidence. In production the same gap is a JSON parser that fails one run in five because temperature was left at 1.0 for a task that needed greedy decoding.

The one core idea— decoding

Every step is four moves. Write z for the raw scores (the logits), one per vocabulary item, T for temperature, and p for the nucleus threshold. Divide z/T, exponentiate and normalize with the softmax, P(i) = e^(zᵢ/T) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ/T)(sigma). Then keep a subset — the top k, or the smallest set summing to at least p. Finally draw one token, or take the largest when decoding greedily.

In one line: temperature reshapes the distribution, truncation removes its tail, and the sampler picks one token from what is left. At T = 1 the distribution is the model's own; at T = 0.5 the top token goes from 0.831 to 0.979; at T = 2 it falls to 0.579.

logits ÷ T → softmax → truncate → renormalize → sample

Temperature changes the shape; top-k and top-p change the support; sampling picks from what survives.

How it actually works— decoding

Six parts: logits, temperature, greedy and beam, top-k, top-p, and the practical settings.

Part 1 — Logits become probabilities

The model's final layer returns one real number per vocabulary item — unbounded, possibly negative, not required to sum to anything. Softmax exponentiates each and divides by the total, producing positive numbers that sum to 1. Only differences matter: add 5 to every logit and the probabilities are unchanged. Why this part exists: the knobs below act either on logits before this step or on probabilities after it, and confusing the two causes wrong arithmetic.

Part 2 — Temperature divides the logits

Replace z with z/T before the softmax. With T < 1 the gaps between logits widen, so the top token takes more probability; with T > 1 the gaps shrink and the distribution flattens. T = 1 is the model's own distribution, and T = 0 is the limit where all mass sits on the argmax, which is why frameworks special-case it as greedy. Why this part exists: it is the only knob that changes probabilities without deleting a token, so tune it first.

Part 3 — Greedy and beam search pick, not sample

Greedy decoding takes the largest probability at every step: deterministic, cheap, and prone to loops, since a repeated "the the the" can be the highest-probability continuation. Beam search keeps the b best partial sequences, which finds higher-probability whole sequences at b times the cost. Why this part exists: high-probability text is not always good text — beam search is strong for translation and bland for open-ended writing.

Part 4 — Top-k keeps a fixed count

Sort by probability, keep the k largest, zero the rest, renormalize. With k = 2 you always draw from two candidates, whether the model was nearly certain or nearly uniform. That constancy is the weakness: k = 50 is too permissive when one token has 0.99 and too restrictive when the top 50 are all plausible. Why this part exists: it is the simplest way to stop sampling from a long tail, and it is still some libraries' default.

Part 5 — Top-p adapts the count

Sort by probability and keep the shortest prefix whose cumulative sum reaches p. If the top token already has 0.95 and p = 0.9, one token survives; if the distribution is flat, dozens do. The set size follows confidence, which is why top-p became the standard for open-ended generation. Why this part exists: it removes the tail without promising a count, so one setting works across confident and uncertain steps.

Part 6 — The rest of the recipe

Repetition penalties subtract from tokens already used; frequency and presence penalties scale with how often and whether they appeared. A seed makes sampling reproducible on the same hardware, and stop sequences end generation at a marker. For extraction, classification and code, use greedy or a very low temperature; for brainstorming, raise T and keep top-p near 0.9. Why this part exists: most "the model is unreliable" reports are a decoding configuration, not a capability.

Read the top row. Temperature 0.5 doubles every logit gap: the 3-versus-1 lead becomes 6-versus-2, and the top token takes 98% of the mass. Temperature 2 halves the gaps: the top token drops to 58% while the second rises from 11% to 21%. Nothing about the model changed; the same four numbers were reshaped. That is why temperature cannot add knowledge — it only redistributes probability the model already assigned.

2. Top-p at work, and where top-k differs

At T = 1, sort the probabilities: 0.831, 0.112, 0.041, 0.015. Cumulative sums are 0.831, 0.943, 0.984, 1.000. With p = 0.9 the shortest prefix reaching 0.9 is the first two, so the survivors are renormalized to 0.831/0.943 = 0.881 and 0.112/0.943 = 0.119.

Now take a flat distribution: 0.30, 0.25, 0.24, 0.21. Top-p 0.9 keeps all four, because 0.30 + 0.25 + 0.24 = 0.79 and the last reaches 1.00. Top-k 2 would keep two. Same threshold, different outcome — the adaptive count is the difference. Renormalization is not optional: after discarding tokens the survivors sum to 0.943, not 1, and a sampler fed 0.943 would occasionally return nothing.

The trap— decoding

Most common wrong mental model

"Temperature makes the model more creative, so a higher value gives better answers." Temperature does not add ideas; it flattens a fixed distribution, giving unlikely tokens more chance. Past a point that buys incoherence, not creativity, and on factual tasks it directly lowers accuracy: the correct token is usually the most probable one, and flattening is a tax on it.

It feels right because raising temperature visibly changes the output, and change reads as novelty. But the model's knowledge was fixed before decoding; a different sample is a different draw from the same list, not new information.

Correction: choose decoding by task. Greedy for extraction, classification and code; low temperature with top-p near 0.9 for summaries; higher temperature only where variety is the goal and a wrong answer is cheap. Report the settings with any sample — a prompt compared at different temperatures is not a controlled test.

The tell: a benchmark where the same prompt gives different scores across runs because no seed was fixed, or an extraction task that succeeds four times out of five at T = 1 and five out of five at T = 0. A second tell is top-p 0.9 keeping 40 tokens on a flat step while keeping one on a confident step — working as designed, but a sign that a fixed top-k would behave very differently. A third is repetition that survives a temperature change but disappears with a repetition penalty.

Retrieval check— decoding

  1. A distribution is 0.5, 0.3, 0.2. What does top-p 0.8 keep, and what are the new probabilities?
    Answer

    The first two: 0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8. Renormalized: 0.625 and 0.375.

  2. Why is temperature 0 defined as greedy rather than as a softmax?
    Answer

    Dividing by zero is undefined, but the limit sends all probability to the largest logit. Greedy implements that limit.

  3. You need identical JSON on every run. Which settings?
    Answer

    Greedy decoding (T = 0) or beam search with a fixed beam. Sampling at any positive temperature is intentionally non-deterministic unless a seed is fixed.

Build it in Colab— decoding

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. Sections, plots and line-by-line notes — run the cells in order, then change the temperature and rerun.

import numpy as np
z = np.array([3.0, 1.0, 0.0, -1.0])        # four candidate logits
for T in (0.5, 1.0, 2.0):                  # three temperatures
    p = np.exp(z / T)                      # scale, then exponentiate
    p = p / p.sum()                        # normalize to a distribution
    print(f"T={T}  top={p[0]:.3f}  second={p[1]:.3f}")
