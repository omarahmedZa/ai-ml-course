Why explainability exists

You can distinguish intrinsic vs post-hoc and local vs global, and name the accountability use case.

Why this matters

A model that outputs 0.31 has not given anyone a reason. Explainability is the practice of producing one: which inputs drove this output, how much each contributed, and what the model would have said without them. You need it whenever a number turns into a decision a human must defend — a loan declined, a scan flagged, a claim denied. It is also the fastest way to debug a model that scores well and behaves absurdly.

0.96AUC at the training hospital
0.61AUC one hospital later
0.31output, with no reason attached

A pneumonia classifier reached AUC 0.96 (area under the ROC curve; 1.0 is perfect) at its training hospital and 0.61 at the next one. One local explanation showed why: the five highest-contributing pixels sat in the burned-in scanner label in the corner. Accuracy could never surface that, because on the training site the label correlated perfectly with pneumonia — the shortcut looked exactly like skill. One attribution map found it in ten seconds.

The one core idea

An explanation is an argument about why an output occurred, and every argument has an audience. The applicant wants the two facts they can change. The officer wants contributions plus a comparable case. The regulator wants error rates by group plus the rule itself. Same prediction, three arguments — and most explainable-AI failures are one audience being handed another audience's argument.

In one line: an explanation is a claim about which inputs the model used, phrased for whoever has to act on it. For an applicant at p = 0.31 against a 0.25 threshold, the applicant's version is four words — "two missed payments" — while the model's own version is six numbers that add to −0.80 in log-odds.

How it actually works

Six parts: three of vocabulary, three of decisions.

explanation = when × scope × form

When it is made (intrinsic or post-hoc), how much it covers (global or local), and what shape it takes (attribution or example). The use case fixes the position.

Part 1 — Importance is always relative

Nothing is important on its own; importance is a difference, so it needs a baseline. A contribution of +0.70 means nothing until you say it is measured against an average applicant, or against this applicant with one field blanked out.

Why this part exists: without a stated baseline, two analysts produce two correct explanations that disagree and neither can be checked.

Part 2 — Intrinsic means the model is the explanation

An intrinsic model is readable by design: linear, shallow tree, short rule list. No second method is needed, because the parameters are the reason — a coefficient of +0.8 on debt-to-income, or a depth-4 tree with at most 2⁴ = 16 readable leaves. The price is capacity: readable models usually predict less well.

Why this part exists: skip intrinsic and you pay for a black box, then pay again to explain it — when a model three AUC points behind might satisfy both auditor and engineer.

Part 3 — Post-hoc means a second method reads the first

Post-hoc means after training: keep the boosted model and run an explainer over it — SHAP, LIME, gradients (topic 31). You keep the accuracy and buy an estimate about a function you cannot read, so the explanation can be wrong in ways the model is not. It also adds a second artefact to monitor, at 40 ms per decision where the intrinsic model costs zero.

Why this part exists: a post-hoc number is a measurement of the model, not a fact about it, and topic 32 exists because measurements need testing.

Part 4 — Global is the model, local is one row

Global describes behaviour across a dataset: debt-to-income carries 28% of average attribution over 5,000 applications. Local describes one row: for applicant 4,182, missed payments added 0.70. Global answers what the model keys on; local answers why it said no to me. They disagree routinely: a feature ranked 4th overall can be 2nd here.

Why this part exists: the commonest wrong deliverable is a global chart handed to someone asking a local question. It is not false, just an answer to a different question.

Part 5 — Timing, scope and form are separate axes

Intrinsic versus post-hoc is when. Global versus local is scope. Attribution versus example-based is form — "income mattered" against "here are three approved applicants like you". Independent axes give 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 positions, and one project can hold several: an intrinsic tree, read globally for the audit and locally for the letter.

Why this part exists: people collapse the axes and argue about whether a model is "interpretable", as if that were one property. Naming the axis turns a debate into a priced choice.

Part 6 — The use case picks the argument

Four uses pull differently. Debugging wants cheap local attribution across many rows, because you are hunting a shortcut. Accountability wants the actual reason for one decision, contestable — GDPR Art. 22 constrains purely automated decisions with significant effects. Trust wants a check a clinician can run in seconds. Safety wants the shortcut found before deployment, when the fix costs days rather than a recall.

Why this part exists: "we need explainability" is not a requirement you can build against; without a use case you cannot tell whether the deliverable is 5,000 rows or one paragraph.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"An explanation tells you the cause." It feels right because the output moves when the feature moves, and that is what a cause does. But post-hoc methods describe the model's function, never the world's causal structure.

Concretely: "income contributed −0.35" means lowering income in the input lowers the score. It does not mean raising a real applicant's income by £10k would lower their risk: the model saw only 5,000 rows, where income also stood in for job stability.

Correction: read every attribution as a claim about the function, with an implicit "holding the rest of the input fixed". Causal claims need an experiment or a causal graph; no explainer produces either.

Second trap: explainability theatre, a heatmap shipped because it looks rigorous. An explanation nobody can act on, or never validated (topic 32), is decoration with a compliance cost.

What it feels likeWhat is true
The output moved when the feature moved, so the explanation names the cause.It names a dependence in the model's function, holding other inputs fixed — never an intervention in the world.

The tell: ask for one counterfactual with a number in it — "what would this applicant have had to change to score below 0.25?" If nobody can answer, you have a picture, not a reason. The second tell is a top feature no domain expert has mentioned; when the model's first reason surprises the people who know the problem, you have found a bug or the project's best insight, and only a check tells them apart.

Retrieval check

  1. A decision tree of depth 4 is used for credit scoring. Is it intrinsic or post-hoc, and local or global?
    Answer

    Intrinsic — the model is the explanation. It supports both local (the path taken) and global (the whole tree) readings.

  2. Why is a global explanation insufficient for an individual rejection?
    Answer

    Global describes average behaviour. The applicant needs the reason for their output, which may be a rare interaction invisible in aggregate importance.

  3. What is the difference between explaining a model and explaining a prediction?
    Answer

    The model explanation characterises the function everywhere; the prediction explanation is a local claim about one input. Methods differ: global importance vs local attribution.
