Why this matters— tokenization

A language model never sees text. It sees a sequence of integer IDs, and the tokenizer is the only component that decides how text becomes those IDs. That choice fixes the sequence length, the size of the embedding matrix, and how much of the context window a document consumes. Two models with identical architecture can differ by a factor of two in cost and effective memory purely because of their tokenizers.

The failure it prevents. An exam asks why "unhappiness" becomes three tokens instead of one. "Because the vocabulary is limited" is a start; the marks come from saying the vocabulary is built from frequent substrings, so a rare word falls back to pieces it shares with common words. In code the same gap is a token count you assumed was words: a 4,000-token budget fills after 2,000 words, and a context window that looked generous overflows halfway through.

The one core idea— tokenization

Start with single characters, then repeatedly glue together the most common neighbouring pair. That is byte-pair encoding (BPE). Write V for the vocabulary — the set of strings the tokenizer can emit — and M for the number of merges you perform. Each merge adds exactly one new symbol to V, so |V| = 256 + M for a byte-level start. The corpus decides which pairs are worth gluing: frequent sequences like th or ing become single tokens, while a rare name stays as several pieces.

In one line: BPE compresses frequent strings into single symbols until the vocabulary hits its budget. A 50,000-merge tokenizer has 50,256 symbols, turns common English words into one or two tokens, and never fails on an unseen word because the 256 single bytes can spell anything.

count pairs → merge the winner → repeat

Frequency picks the merge; the vocabulary budget stops it. Encoding replays the learned merges in rank order.

How it actually works— tokenization

Six parts: the base symbols, the counting, the merge, the stopping rule, the encoding, and the trade-off.

Part 1 — Start from bytes

A byte-level tokenizer begins with the 256 possible byte values, so every UTF-8 string is representable. A character-level tokenizer begins with the alphabet it saw, plus an unknown token. Bytes never produce an unknown token; characters produce one whenever a new script appears. Why this part exists: the base set is what makes fallback possible, and it sets the floor for the vocabulary size.

Part 2 — Count adjacent pairs, weighted by frequency

Split every training word into symbols, then count each adjacent pair. If "low" appears three times and "lower" twice, (l, o) gets a count of 5. Counting word types instead of occurrences would over-weight rare words. Why this part exists: the count decides what becomes a token, so a corpus unlike the target text gives the wrong vocabulary.

Part 3 — Merge the winner

Take the highest-count pair, replace every occurrence with a new symbol, and record the merge with its rank. (l, o) becomes lo; the next count sees lo as one unit. Ties are broken deterministically — often by taking the lexicographically smallest pair — so the same corpus always gives the same vocabulary. Why this part exists: this is the learning step; without a fixed tie-break, two runs on the same data produce different token IDs.

Part 4 — Stop at the vocabulary budget

Repeat until the vocabulary reaches its target size. A budget of 50,000 merges gives 50,256 byte-level symbols. More merges mean longer tokens and shorter sequences, but a larger embedding matrix and a larger output layer. Why this part exists: this is the one dial you set before training, and it cannot be changed afterwards without retraining the model.

Part 5 — Encoding replays the merges by rank

To tokenize new text, split it into bytes and apply the learned merges in rank order, earliest first. The result depends on the ranks, not on finding the longest match. The same word with a leading space is usually a different token. Why this part exists: encoding must match the merges used at training time, or the IDs mean nothing.

Part 6 — The trade-off you are actually choosing

Vocabulary size |V| and sequence length n trade against each other. The embedding matrix costs |V| × d parameters, where d is the model width, while attention costs grow with n². A 50,000-token vocabulary with d = 768 is 38.4 million embedding parameters; a 256-token vocabulary would shrink that to 0.2 million but triple the sequence length. Why this part exists: tokenizer choice is an architecture decision with a measurable parameter and compute bill.

Worked example— tokenization

1. Four merges on a five-word corpus

The corpus is "low" three times and "lower" twice, written without spaces for clarity. Start from characters, so the initial symbol count is 3 × 3 + 2 × 5 = 19.

1(l,o): 5 · (o,w): 5 · (w,e): 2 · (e,r): 2lolo w / lo w e r
2(lo,w): 5 · (w,e): 2 · (e,r): 2lowlow / low e r
3(low,e): 2 · (e,r): 2erlow / low er
4(low,er): 2lowerlow / lower

Read the table as a sentence. After merge 1, the five occurrences of "low" share the symbol lo, so the corpus drops from 19 symbols to 14. Merge 2 makes low, dropping to 9. Merge 3 has a tie at 2; the rule picks er, giving 7. Merge 4 makes lower, giving 5 tokens — exactly one per word occurrence. The vocabulary is 256 + 4 = 260 symbols.

Now encode a word that was never in the corpus. "lowest" starts as l o w e s t. Merge 1 applies to l o, giving lo w e s t. Merge 2 applies to lo w, giving low e s t. Merges 3 and 4 need e r and low er, neither of which appears, so encoding stops at four tokens. Nothing failed; the word was covered by pieces.

2. What the budget costs

Take a 50,000-merge tokenizer and a model width of d = 768. The embedding matrix has one row per symbol: 50,256 × 768 = 38,596,608 parameters, about 38.6 million. If the same text averages 1.3 tokens per word, a 3,000-word document is roughly 3,900 tokens — already most of a 4,096-token context window.

A 10,000-merge tokenizer would have 10,256 rows and 7.9 million embedding parameters, but the same document might take 5,200 tokens and no longer fit. That is the trade in one comparison: 30 million parameters saved against 1,300 extra positions of context spent.

The trap— tokenization

Most common wrong mental model

"A token is a word, so token counts and word counts are the same." A token is a substring chosen by frequency. Common words are one token, rare words are several, numbers often split digit by digit, and a leading space is part of the token. The ratio is language-dependent: English averages about 1.3 tokens per word, while Hindi or Thai can exceed 3, so the same context window holds far less text.

It feels right because tokenizers demo beautifully on English prose, where most words are indeed one token. The gap appears on names, code, numbers and non-English text — exactly the inputs where a silent truncation costs you the answer.

Correction: measure in tokens, not words. Before trusting a context budget, tokenize a sample of the real input and look at the distribution of tokens per document; if the tail exceeds the window, chunk deliberately instead of truncating silently.

The tell: an estimated cost that is 40% low, or a prompt that "should fit" being cut off mid-sentence. A second is a model that spells out arithmetic digit by digit while handling words fluently — the tokenizer split the number. A third is a multilingual benchmark collapsing on one language at equal word counts.

Retrieval check— tokenization

  1. Why does byte-level BPE never need an unknown token?
    Answer

    Its base vocabulary is all 256 byte values, and every UTF-8 string is a sequence of bytes. Any input can be spelled from single bytes.

  2. After 8,000 merges from a byte-level start, what is the vocabulary size?
    Answer

    8,256: the 256 bytes plus one symbol per merge. Special tokens such as end-of-text would add to that.

  3. Two tokenizers have vocabularies of 32k and 64k. What happens to sequence length and embedding parameters?
    Answer

    The 64k one gives shorter sequences but roughly doubles the embedding matrix. The compute saving depends on how much the sequence shortens.

Solve it · 048 — Most common adjacent pair

Build it in Colab— tokenization

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. Sections, plots and line-by-line notes — run the cells in order, then change the corpus and rerun.

from collections import Counter
words = {"low": 3, "lower": 2}                 # word -> frequency
tokens = {tuple(w): f for w, f in words.items()}  # split into characters
pairs = Counter()                              # adjacent pair counts
for symbols, f in tokens.items():
    for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
        pairs[(a, b)] += f                     # weight by word frequency
print(pairs.most_common(1))                    # (('l', 'o'), 5)
