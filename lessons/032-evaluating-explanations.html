Evaluating explanations

You can test faithfulness and explain the correlation ≠ causation trap.

Why this matters

An explanation that looks convincing and is wrong does more damage than no explanation: it turns a black box into false confidence and gives the confidence a diagram. Evaluating explanations is the quality control that the rest of this unit otherwise lacks, and it is the difference between shipping a heatmap and shipping evidence.

A team built a saliency dashboard for a radiology model and had two clinicians review the maps. Both approved. Then somebody ran a deletion test: hiding the top 10% of highlighted pixels dropped AUC from 0.94 to 0.91, exactly the same as hiding a random tenth of the pixels. Six weeks of work, and the map was an edge detector — attractive, smooth, and unconnected to the model's decision.

The one core idea

Faithfulness asks whether an explanation reflects the model's actual computation. Plausibility asks whether a human finds it convincing. The two are measured in completely different ways — a perturbation experiment versus an opinion — and they disagree often enough that you must test the first and cannot infer it from the second.

In one line: an explanation is a prediction about what happens when you remove those inputs, so test it the way you would test any prediction. Remove the top 10% of pixels: a faithful map falls to AUC 0.71, a decorative one to 0.90, and random deletion to 0.91. Only the first number is evidence.

How it actually works

Six parts: one claim, four tests, and one way the tests get gamed.

Part 1 — Faithfulness is a claim about removal

An explanation says which inputs the model used. That is a testable prediction: take those inputs away and the output should fall. Plausibility is a different property — whether the story sounds like something a human would believe. A smooth outline of a cat's ear is plausible and may reflect nothing at all.

Why this part exists: without the split, review becomes a taste test, and the prettiest map wins regardless of whether it describes the model.

Part 2 — Deletion: take the top-k away and watch the score

Rank features by attribution, delete the top 10%, and re-measure. A faithful ranking has named load-bearing inputs, so the metric collapses. On 5,000 test images, method A falls from AUC 0.94 to 0.71, while random deletion of the same count falls only to 0.91.

Why this part exists: it tests the claim head-on, and it needs nothing but a re-run of the model you already own.

Part 3 — Insertion: add them back and watch recovery

Insertion runs the ranking backwards: start from a blank input, where AUC sits near 0.50 — chance, on balanced data — and add features from least to most important. A faithful ranking recovers the prediction within the first few steps; a decorative one needs almost the whole image.

Why this part exists: deletion alone rewards a map that names everything important; insertion forces the ranking to be ordered, not merely a set.

Part 4 — Randomise the weights

Replace the trained weights with random ones, keep the architecture, recompute the explanation. If the map barely changes, it was reading input statistics — edges, contrast, brightness — rather than the model. Adebayo et al. (2018) found several widely used saliency methods fail exactly this check.

Why this part exists: it is the only test that separates "describes this model" from "describes images", and the two look identical on a screen.

Part 5 — Perturb the input and re-run

Shift the input by one pixel, or resample the explainer, and compare the two outputs. A stable method returns attributions that move a little; an unstable one reorders its top five. LIME under two seeds (topic 31) is the standard example, and the flip there was from 0.42 to 0.05.

Why this part exists: an explanation that changes under noise cannot support a decision that has to stand for months in a file.

Part 6 — Watch for explanation hacking

If the model is trained with a penalty on its explanations, it can learn to emit clean maps while computing something else, because the penalty sits on the map and not on the mechanism. Keep the explainer out of the training loop, or hold back a test set that nobody tuned against and run deletion there.

Why this part exists: once the explanation becomes an objective, it stops being a measurement; Goodhart's law applies to heatmaps as readily as to accuracy.

Worked example

1. Deletion, two methods, one random baseline

Image classifier, 5,000 test images, clean AUC 0.94. Delete features in attribution order and re-measure.

RemovedMethod AAUC lostMethod BAUC lostRandom
Top 10%0.710.230.900.040.91
Top 30%0.380.560.820.120.85

What to notice. Method A loses 0.94 − 0.71 = 0.23 at 10%, nearly eight times the random loss of 0.03, so its top pixels really are load-bearing. Method B loses 0.04 — worse than chance, since random deletion costs 0.03 — so its "important" pixels are decoration. Plausibility would have ranked B first: its maps were smoother and more localised.

2. Insertion, as a fraction of the gap

The same two rankings, added back from a blank input where AUC is 0.50. The gap to close is 0.94 − 0.50 = 0.44.

AddedA AUCA recoveryB AUCB recovery
Least 70%, then top 10%0.860.36 / 0.44 = 82%0.610.11 / 0.44 = 25%
Then top 30%0.930.43 / 0.44 = 98%0.740.24 / 0.44 = 55%

What to notice. The two tests agree, which is the point of running both. A recovers 82% of the gap from a tenth of the pixels and 98% from under a third; B reaches 25% and then 55%, needing most of the image to get halfway. Delete-then-insert is one experiment with two directions, and disagreement between them usually means the ranking is fine at the top and scrambled below it.

3. The randomisation check, counted

Retrain with shuffled labels, recompute both maps, and count how many of the original top 20 pixels survive.

What to notice. B keeps almost everything when the model learns nothing, which means the map never depended on what was learned. That single number — 95% — would have saved the six weeks described at the top of this lesson, and it takes one retrain to compute.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"A domain expert found the explanation convincing, so it is validated." It feels right because expert review is the obvious quality gate, and it is the gate we use for everything else in a project. But an expert reading a heatmap is judging plausibility, and humans are systematically fooled by maps that are smooth, sparse and localised — the three properties that make a picture look like an explanation.

Faithfulness is not visible on a screen. It is a number produced by removing something and measuring what happens, and no amount of agreement changes that number.

Correction: accept an explanation only with a perturbation result attached — deletion, insertion, or randomisation — on data nobody tuned against. Review the experiment, then the picture.

Second trap: reading attention as attribution. "The model attended here" and "this caused the decision" are different claims; attention is one correlational summary of a single forward pass (topic 26).

The tell: a validation slide whose evidence is a quote from a clinician instead of a number from a deletion run. The second tell is an explanation that was never recomputed after the model was retrained — if the map did not change when the weights did, it was never describing the weights.

Retrieval check

  1. What is the difference between faithfulness and plausibility?
    Answer

    Faithfulness: the explanation reflects the model's actual computation, testable by perturbation. Plausibility: it matches human intuition. A method can score high on one and low on the other.

  2. Why does the model-randomisation test catch fake saliency?
    Answer

    If the weights are randomised, the learned features are gone. An explanation that ignores this is responding to input statistics (edges, contrast), not to the model.

  3. You remove the top 5 features and accuracy does not change. What do you conclude?
    Answer

    The attribution is not faithful for this model — those features were not necessary for the prediction. Either the method is wrong, or the model is using redundant features.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_features = 20
true_importance = np.zeros(n_features)
true_importance[:3] = [3., 2., 1.]
X = rng.normal(size=(500, n_features))
y = (X @ true_importance > 0).astype(int)
