Non-IID data & client drift

You can explain client drift, name the fixes, and state the local-steps trade-off.

Why this matters

Non-IID data is the normal condition of every real deployment: one phone belongs to a teenager who types in Portuguese, the next to a radiologist who types in English. Papers usually demonstrate on an evenly split dataset where every client sees the same ten classes; that split is the one thing a real fleet never gives you.

In an exam the question is sharp: "does raising the number of local epochs improve communication efficiency?" The answer is no in general — it cuts rounds but grows drift — and answering "yes, fewer rounds" is the standard half-mark answer. In a project the failure is quieter: you raise E from 1 to 50 to cut the bandwidth bill, total accuracy falls two points, you blame the learning rate, and the drop sits in the three clients with the least data. Nothing in the code is wrong; the assumption was.

The one core idea

Two hikers are roped together but walking to different villages. Each step takes a hiker toward their own village, the rope pulls both back toward the middle, and after enough steps they are standing in a field that is neither village. Neither has arrived, and the rope is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

In one line: local training moves each client toward its own optimum, and the average of several different optima is a point that is optimal for nobody. Client A's optimum is −1 and client B's is +1; after 10 local epochs they sit at −0.9 and +0.9, and the server averages them to 0.0.

How it actually works

Six mechanisms turn that picture into a failure you can measure. IID means independent and identically distributed: every client's rows drawn from the same underlying mix.

Part 1 — IID is the assumption being broken

Under IID, each client's 1,000 examples are a fair sample of the same ten-class mix, so its local gradient is an unbiased estimate of the global one, and averaging those is safe. Non-IID means that guarantee has lapsed.

Why this part exists: without naming the assumption you cannot say what drift is a violation of, and every fix — reweighting, proximal terms, personalisation — is a repair to this one line.

Part 2 — Label skew is the common case

Client A has only digit 3, client B only digit 7. Each local model becomes extremely confident on one of ten classes and has effectively never seen the other eight. The local optimum is not a mild bias; it is a model that has been trained to answer a different question.

Why this part exists: averaging two models that are confidently wrong in opposite directions gives a model that is uncertain everywhere, so global accuracy can fall while every client improved locally.

Part 3 — Quantity skew bends the average

One client holds 10 examples and another 100,000, so their shares are 0.0001 and 0.9999. The large client's optimum effectively is the global model; the small one is a rounding error in its own training run.

Why this part exists: a model can be accurate on average and useless for the client the deployment was built for, and the averaging rule cannot distinguish "small because rare" from "small because unimportant".

Part 4 — Local steps amplify the divergence

Each local epoch moves a client further toward its private optimum before the server sees anything: E = 1 to E = 10 takes the distance travelled from 0.2 to 0.9 below. The average is taken after that divergence, never before.

Why this part exists: this is the topic's core trade-off: local steps save rounds and cost agreement, and the bill arrives in a currency the loss curve never shows.

Part 5 — FedProx puts a leash on each step

FedProx adds (μ/2)‖w − w_t‖² to each local objective, so the local gradient becomes ∇F_k(w) + μ(w − w_t). That term is zero at the global model and grows with distance: a spring tied to w_t. μ is the stiffness — 0 removes the leash, large μ approaches no local steps at all.

Why this part exists: without a leash, more local work is unbounded drift; with one, a client can take many steps without the server losing control of where it ends up.

Part 6 — Personalisation and fairness

Sometimes the honest response is to stop chasing one global model: share a representation, then fine-tune the last layers per client, or keep batch-norm statistics local (FedBN). Report accuracy per client as well as the mean; a mean of 95% across 1,000 clients is compatible with 40% on the smallest.

Why this part exists: a single aggregate number hides exactly the clients that non-IID data hurts, and those are usually the users the system was deployed to serve.

2. Why the average can miss the pooled optimum

Give the two clients bowls of different widths: F(w) = ½[4(w + 1)² + (w − 1)²]. The local optima are still −1 and +1, so plain averaging still produces 0.0. Differentiate to find the pooled optimum:

What to notice. Averaging local optima costs 56% more pooled loss, 2.5 against 1.6, with no warning: both clients converged and the average is arithmetically correct. The gap is zero when the bowls share a width, which is why the evenly-split benchmark hides it.

One client with loss ½(w + 1)², starting from w_t = 0. Setting (w + 1) + μw = 0 gives the fixed point w = −1/(1 + μ).

What to notice. The leash changes how far the client gets, not which way it wants to go, and it moves on a smooth curve rather than a switch. That is what lets you raise E without raising drift by the same factor: at μ = 4 the client travels 0.2 instead of 1.0.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"More local epochs always improve communication efficiency." It feels right because local epochs do reduce round count, and round count is the number everyone plots on the x-axis, usually stopping at E = 10 where the curve still looks flat.

Two quantities move in opposite directions: rounds fall roughly as 1/E, while drift grows with E until the average stops being useful. Past a small value the accuracy falls faster than the saving grows, so the cost to reach a target accuracy rises. The optimum is empirical, never monotone.

Correction: tune E and μ together against a target accuracy, and always report the number of rounds needed to reach it — not rounds per unit of local work. A second trap: evaluating only on a pooled test set, where the average client dominates and the smallest client disappears.

The tell: accuracy that drops as you increase E while training loss on the pooled set keeps falling — that combination means each client is fitting its own corner of the data. The second tell is a single global accuracy number in a report that never breaks results down by client; if the smallest client is not shown, its 40% is inside the 95%.

Retrieval check

  1. What is client drift, in one sentence?
    Answer

    Local updates move each client's weights toward its own data distribution, so the averaged global model sits in a region that is optimal for no client.

  2. What does the proximal term in FedProx do as μ increases?
    Answer

    It penalises distance from the global weights more heavily, shrinking each client's step toward its local optimum. Large μ behaves more like federated SGD.

  3. Why might per-client accuracy be the right metric instead of the global average?
    Answer

    Because FL is usually deployed to serve each client. A high mean can hide a small or unusual client being systematically misclassified — a fairness failure invisible in aggregate.
