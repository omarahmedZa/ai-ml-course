DQN: two fixes that made it work

You can explain replay buffer and target network as fixes for correlated data and moving targets.

Why this matters

DQN is the canonical example of "the algorithm was known for decades; the engineering made it work". It is also the cleanest exam question in Unit 6: name the two tricks and the problem each solves.

Every value-based deep RL system you will meet is DQN with the serial numbers filed off — including the critic inside most modern agents. Two failures it removes, and you will meet both. The exam failure: "Your Q-network's loss drops for a while, then the average return collapses. Name a likely cause and the fix." One line on correlated frames and one on a moving target earns the marks; "tune the learning rate" earns none. The project failure: you train on consecutive frames, the agent memorises one corridor, reward spikes, then falls off a cliff — and because the loss looked healthy the whole time, you keep training and burn a day of GPU.

The one core idea

Two separate faults need two separate repairs. The data you learn from must look like a shuffled deck rather than a film played in order; the answer key you mark against must be printed and pinned to the wall rather than rewritten each time you look at it. Neither repair changes what Q-learning is trying to compute.

In one line: shuffle a million transitions, and freeze the answer key for 10,000 steps. A batch of 32 consecutive frames holds roughly 1 distinct situation; 32 draws from a 1,000,000-transition buffer hold 32.

How it actually works

Six parts follow; each repairs one fault, and later parts assume the earlier repairs hold.

Part 1 — Two faults, not one

Q-learning with a network has two independent defects. Data arrives in time order, so a gradient step sees the same corridor 32 times; and the target r + γ max Q_θ(s′) is written in the same weights the step is about to change. Why this part exists: naming them separately is what makes the two fixes, and the exam answer, fall out.

Part 2 — Replay buffer: store, then shuffle

Keep recent transitions in a buffer of 10⁶ and draw random minibatches of 32. A stored transition stays usable, so one frame can teach many updates; the buffer is a ring, evicting the oldest entry at 10⁶, so the data never lags the policy by more than a million steps. Why this part exists: random sampling restores the approximate i.i.d. assumption the gradient expects — without it, 32 near-identical frames act as one sample holding the whole batch's weight.

Part 3 — Target network: freeze a copy

Keep a second weight vector θ⁻ and copy θ into it only every 10,000 steps. Targets come from θ⁻; gradients flow into θ. Between copies the target is a constant, so the update becomes ordinary regression. Why this part exists: a constant target is what lets the loss actually fall — example 2 shows the alternative in numbers.

Part 4 — Huber loss: cap the outlier

Below an error of 1 the Huber loss is 0.5 × error²; above it the loss is |error| − 0.5, so a TD error of 50 contributes about 50 of gradient instead of 2,500. Big errors come from rare rewards and from targets still catching up. Why this part exists: one unclipped outlier can undo a thousand careful updates in a single step.

Part 5 — Double DQN: pick and price separately

Standard DQN takes a maximum over θ⁻'s noisy estimates, and the maximum of noise sits above the truth. Double DQN chooses the action with θ, then reads that action's value from θ⁻. Why this part exists: it splits choosing from scoring, removing most of the upward bias for one extra forward pass.

Part 6 — Two policies, one network

The behaviour policy generates data: ε-greedy on Q_θ. The target policy is what you are learning: pure greedy max. ε decays 1.0 → 0.1 over the first million frames, and 4 stacked frames span 4 ÷ 60 ≈ 0.067 s so the network can see velocity. Why this part exists: one frame cannot tell "moving left" from "moving right", and greedy behaviour too early starves the buffer of variety.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"A replay buffer is a general RL trick — more data is always better." It feels right because the buffer is just memory, and memory sounds neutral. But every stored transition was produced by a policy, and reusing it is only valid if your gradient tolerates data from an older policy.

DQN tolerates it because its target is a max over actions, which needs no probability from the behaviour policy. Put a buffer behind REINFORCE or PPO and the importance ratio π_new / π_old spans orders of magnitude; the gradient you compute is for a policy that no longer exists, and the weights move in a direction nothing supports.

Correction: replay is an off-policy tool. On-policy methods may reuse a rollout for a few epochs, then must discard it. A second trap: Double DQN reduces the bias from the max; it does not make estimates unbiased.

The tell: the ratio histogram in your logs has a tail past 10, or the entropy collapses in a few hundred steps while the loss looks fine. For the moving-target fault the tell is different: loss falls smoothly, evaluation return rises and then breaks, and reloading an older checkpoint recovers the run — a target that outran its own predictions.

Retrieval check

  1. What does the target network freeze, and for how long?
    Answer

    The weights used to compute max Q(s′,a′). They are copied from the online network every N steps (commonly 10k), then held fixed while the online network trains.

  2. Why does a replay buffer improve sample efficiency?
    Answer

    Every stored transition can be reused in many updates instead of being discarded after one gradient step. It also decorrelates batches, which stabilises the fit.

  3. Why can't PPO use a replay buffer the same way?
    Answer

    PPO is on-policy: its gradient is only valid for data from the current policy. Reusing old-policy data requires importance sampling, and the ratio variance grows quickly with policy drift.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. Sections, plots, sliders and line-by-line notes — run the cells in order, then change one number and rerun.

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
buffer, r = [], 0.0
for _ in range(1000):
    s = rng.normal(size=4)
    r = 0.9 * r + rng.normal()          # temporally correlated reward
    buffer.append((s, rng.integers(2), r, s + rng.normal(size=4) * .1))
