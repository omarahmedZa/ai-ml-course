Bandits beyond ε (epsilon)-greedy

You can compute a UCB index by hand, explain Thompson sampling, and say why regret grows linearly under ε-greedy.

Reinforcement LearningRL

Why this matters— the trade

A bandit is the smallest reinforcement-learning problem: one state, several actions, and a reward you see only for the action you took. Choosing an ad, a treatment, or a headline is a bandit. The question is always: exploit the best-looking arm, or explore one that might be better?

The failure it prevents. With 10 arms and 1,000 pulls, ε = 0.1 spends about 100 pulls on uniformly random arms. If 9 are bad, roughly 90 of those pulls buy nothing. The loss is the policy, which explores at a fixed rate no matter what it already knows.

The one core idea— optimism

Each arm has an unknown mean reward μ_a(mu). After n_a pulls you have an estimate μ̂_a, and a mean from 3 pulls is worth less than one from 300. Good exploration spends pulls where the estimate is uncertain and could plausibly be the best — not uniformly across all arms.

In one line: act as if every arm were as good as its most optimistic plausible value. Upper confidence bound (UCB) adds an explicit bonus that shrinks as an arm is sampled; Thompson sampling draws a whole plausible mean from a posterior and acts on the draw.

index(a) = μ̂_a + √( 2 ln t / n_a )

Mean plus a bonus. The bonus falls as n_a grows, so an arm must eventually prove itself or lose.

How it actually works— the index

Five parts, then one assumption to check.

Part 1 — Keep a mean and a count per arm

After each pull, update n_a ← n_a + 1 and μ̂_a ← μ̂_a + (r − μ̂_a) / n_a. Two numbers per arm are the whole memory. Why this part exists: the count is what tells the method how much it does not know.

Part 2 — Pull every arm once

An arm with n_a = 0 gets an infinite index, so UCB tries each arm before comparing. Why this part exists: with no observation there is no estimate, and dividing by zero is not a policy.

Part 3 — Add the shrinking bonus

At step t, arm a scores μ̂_a + √(2 ln t / n_a)(square root). The numerator grows like log time, slowly; the denominator grows with pulls, quickly. Why this part exists: the bonus stands in for "how far above its mean could this arm be", and shrinks exactly when evidence arrives.

Part 4 — Or sample from a posterior

For a 0/1 reward, keep Beta(α, β)(alpha) with α = 1 + successes and β = 1 + failures(beta). Draw one value per arm, pick the largest, and update only the arm you pulled. Why this part exists: sampling explores in proportion to the probability that an arm is best, without banning any arm outright.

Part 5 — Measure regret, not just reward

Regret after T pulls is T μ* − Σ r_t(sigma), where μ* is the best arm's true mean. Why this part exists: average reward hides the cost of exploration; regret makes it the quantity being minimised.

What to notice. Arms 1 and 3 tie at 0.98 despite different means, because their bonuses compensate: arm 3 is better but better known. Arm 2 wins at 1.56 with only 10 pulls. An index above 1.0 is impossible for a probability — it is an optimistic score, not an estimate.

2. What happens after 40 more pulls

Arm 2 is pulled 40 more times and its mean falls to 0.58. Now n = 50, so its bonus is 0.43 and its index is 0.58 + 0.43 = 1.01. Arm 1, untouched, sits near 0.98. Arm 2 stays ahead, but the gap has collapsed from 0.58 to 0.03: evidence shrinks the bonus, and the arm must hold its lead on merit.

3. Regret for 1,000 pulls

Suppose the best arm truly pays 0.60 and a policy earns 0.54 on average. Regret is 1,000 × (0.60 − 0.54) = 60 reward units, which reads as "60 pulls wasted". Under ε-greedy with ε = 0.1 and 9 suboptimal arms, nearly every exploratory pull is wasted, so those pulls keep accumulating: about 90 of every 1,000 pulls are wasted. UCB's bonus shrinks, so its regret grows like ln T.

Why the logarithm. The bonus falls as 1/√n, so an arm needs exponentially more pulls to earn the same reduction. Sampling continues where it might change the decision, and stops where it cannot.

The trap— the assumption

Most common wrong mental model

"Exploration means pulling every arm equally." Uniform exploration is the crudest option, and it keeps paying for information you already have. Exploration is only worth buying where it could change the decision.

It feels right because ε-greedy comes first and a fixed ε looks principled. But the rate cannot tell 5 pulls from 500; it spends the same 10% on both, and its regret is linear in T.

Correction: exploration should be proportional to uncertainty and to the chance that the uncertain arm is best. UCB does this with a bonus; Thompson does it by sampling a posterior. Both reduce pulls on arms that are probably bad.

The tell: cumulative regret is a straight line rather than a flattening curve. A second tell is one arm taking 900 of 1,000 pulls while its interval is still wide — early luck taken for evidence. A third is a non-stationary bandit: UCB assumes fixed means, so a small bonus keeps an old arm on top after it stopped paying.

Retrieval check— recall

  1. Why does an unpulled arm get an infinite UCB index?
    Answer

    With n_a = 0 there is no evidence and the bonus is undefined; treating it as infinite forces one trial of every arm.

  2. Arm A has mean 0.6 from 100 pulls; arm B has mean 0.55 from 4. Which does UCB prefer, and why?
    Answer

    Usually B: its bonus is much larger because 4 pulls leave real uncertainty. UCB is optimistic about the unknown.

  3. What does a Thompson sample represent?
    Answer

    One plausible value of the arm's true mean, drawn from the current posterior. Acting on the largest draw automatically balances exploit and explore.

Solve it · 054 — UCB with unpulled arms

Build it in Colab— the notebook

Runnable, NumPy-only: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. It runs UCB and ε-greedy on the same three arms and plots cumulative regret.

import numpy as np
means = np.array([0.50, 0.60, 0.55])
counts = np.zeros(3); values = np.zeros(3)
for t in range(1, 101):
    bonus = np.sqrt(2 * np.log(t) / np.maximum(counts, 1))
    a = int(np.argmax(values + bonus))   # optimistic choice
    r = np.random.rand() < means[a]
    counts[a] += 1
    values[a] += (r - values[a]) / counts[a]
