Why this matters— margins

A support vector machine answers a question every classifier faces: when many boundaries separate the data, which one should you ship? Its answer is the one with the widest empty strip on either side. That rule gives the model a property no logistic regression has — the final boundary is decided by a handful of awkward examples, not by all of them. Expressed through a kernel, the same idea handles curved boundaries without building curved features by hand.

The failure it prevents. Two models can both score 100% on the training set while behaving differently on Monday's email. One grazes the nearest negative example; the other sits halfway between the classes. An exam question asks exactly this: "Both lines separate the training data — which do you prefer and why?" "The wider margin" is the answer, and it needs a number, not an adjective.

The one core idea— margins

Draw the separating line, then push it away from the nearest point of each class until it cannot move without touching one. The strip it sweeps out is the margin(margin), and the points it touches are the support vectors. Every other point could be deleted and the line would not move. Training is therefore not "fit all 200 emails"; it is "find the line whose nearest examples are as far away as possible".

In one line: maximise the empty strip, and the answer depends only on the points at its edge. A kernel replaces the plain dot product with a similarity score, so the same rule applies in a space with more dimensions — without computing coordinates there.

widest street → few deciding points

The margin is a safety buffer, and the support vectors are the only rows that price it.

How it actually works— margins

Four ideas carry the whole method: the score, the constraint, the margin width, and the kernel. Take them in that order.

Part 1 — A score and a sign

For an input x, the model computes f(x) = w · x + b. Here w is the weight vector — one number per feature — b is the bias, and w · x is the dot product: multiply matching entries and add. The predicted class is +1 when the score is positive and −1 when it is negative. The boundary is the set of points scoring zero, and w points perpendicular to it, which is why its length controls the strip.

Why this matters: the sign is the prediction; the size of the score is the confidence.

Part 2 — The constraint that defines the strip

Encoding the classes as y = +1 and y = −1 lets one inequality cover both sides: y(w · x + b) ≥ 1. For a positive point this says "score at least +1"; for a negative point it says "score at most −1". Points exactly on the dashed lines satisfy it with equality — those are the support vectors. The fixed 1 is what makes the margin a real distance; without it, scaling w and b would satisfy the inequality forever.

Part 3 — The margin is a length

The gap between the dashed lines is 2 / ‖w‖, where ‖w‖ = √(w₁² + w₂² + …)(square root) is the length of the weight vector. Widening the strip therefore means shrinking ‖w‖. Training solves

minimise ½‖w‖² subject to yᵢ(w · xᵢ + b) ≥ 1 for every i

The half and the square make the objective bowl-shaped without changing where its minimum sits. This is the hard-margin SVM. Real data overlaps, so the soft-margin version adds a slack ξᵢ ≥ 0 per point and a price C: minimise ½‖w‖² + C Σξᵢ(sigma). A small C tolerates violations and buys a wide strip; a huge C insists on classifying everything and produces a narrow, wiggly one.

Part 4 — The kernel trick

If no straight boundary works, map the inputs into a richer space and draw a straight boundary there. A kernel K(x, z) returns the dot product of two points after that mapping, without performing it: K(x, z) = φ(x) · φ(z)(phi). The standard choices are the polynomial kernel K(x, z) = (1 + x · z)^d and the radial basis function K(x, z) = exp(−γ‖x − z‖²)(gamma), where γ sets how fast similarity fades with distance.

Why this works: in the dual form of the optimisation, the data appears only inside dot products. Replace every dot product with K and the solver never needs the mapped coordinates. The prediction becomes f(x) = Σᵢ αᵢ yᵢ K(xᵢ, x) + b(alpha), where αᵢ is a weight per training point. Most αᵢ come out zero; the nonzero ones are the support vectors.

Worked example— margins

1. A one-dimensional margin, solved by hand

Two points, one feature. A negative example at x = −1 and a positive one at x = +1. By symmetry the boundary belongs at zero, so b = 0. The support vectors must score exactly +1 and −1:

Read it as a sentence. The points are two units apart, so the widest separating strip fills the whole gap: width 2. The model f(x) = x puts the edges at ±1, exactly on the points.

Now score a third point. Take x = 0.5, a positive example. Its score is 0.5: the class is right, but 0.5 < 1, so it sits inside the strip. Its hinge loss is max(0, 1 − y f(x)) = max(0, 1 − 0.5) = 0.5. A point at x = 1.4 costs max(0, 1 − 1.4) = 0; a positive point at x = −0.2 scores −0.2 and costs 1 − (−0.2) = 1.2.

Average the three. (0.5 + 0 + 1.2) / 3 = 0.567, and training moves w and b to push that number down. Correct and far outside costs nothing; correct inside costs a little; wrong costs more than 1.

Read it as a sentence. At γ = 1, a point one unit away is 37% similar; at γ = 4, it is 2% similar, so each support vector influences only its own neighbourhood. Small γ gives a smooth boundary; large γ lets it curl around single points.

What to notice. The kernel never moves the data. It changes the similarity score, and therefore the boundary's shape. C and γ trade capacity in opposite directions.

The trap— margins

Most common wrong mental model

"A kernel lets the SVM separate anything, so turn γ up until training accuracy is 100%." A kernel does not add information. It re-expresses distances, and a large γ makes every training point its own tiny island: the boundary wraps around each one, training accuracy hits 100%, and test data meets a model that has memorised nothing useful.

It feels right because the training curve responds exactly as promised — more γ, fewer training errors. But what is maximised is the margin in a space defined by γ; shrink the reach far enough and every point's margin is satisfied alone.

Correction: γ and C are capacity dials, not accuracy dials. Tune them on held-out data, and read the support-vector count as a symptom: when nearly every row is one, the model is fitting individual points.

The tell: training accuracy 100%, test accuracy near chance, and a support-vector count close to the number of rows. A healthy RBF model on a few hundred rows keeps a modest fraction; "almost all of them" is the signature of too much capacity.

Retrieval check— margins

  1. Which training points determine the final boundary, and what happens if you delete one of the others?
    Answer

    The support vectors — points on or inside the margin, with nonzero α. Deleting any other point leaves the boundary unchanged, because its constraint was never active.

  2. A linear SVM cannot separate XOR. What exactly does a kernel add?
    Answer

    It adds features implicitly. A degree-2 polynomial kernel includes x₁x₂, and XOR becomes linearly separable. The boundary is straight in the richer space, curved in the original one.

  3. What does C control, and which way does the bias–variance trade go as it rises?
    Answer

    C prices violations of the margin. Small C: wide strip, more mistakes allowed, higher bias. Large C: narrow strip, few mistakes allowed, higher variance.

Build it in Colab— margins

Runnable, NumPy-only: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. It computes a margin, builds an RBF kernel matrix, and plots how γ changes similarity.

import numpy as np
X = np.array([[1., 0.], [0., 1.], [2., 2.]])   # three rows, two features
d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)   # squared distances
K = np.exp(-1.0 * d2)                          # RBF kernel, gamma = 1
print(np.round(K, 3))
