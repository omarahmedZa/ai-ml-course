Monitoring, one number at a time · Lab 1

D (KS distance) — the largest gap between two windows

You can read a drift statistic against its alert line, and say what crossing it does and does not tell you.

Where this sits

D = maxx | Ftrain(x) − Flive(x) |

Lab 1 · F is a cumulative share; only the live window is allowed to move

Monitoring keeps one distribution still. Ftrain is the reference — the scores from a known-good week, frozen as a file. Flive is the window you are watching now. D is the largest vertical gap between the two cumulative curves: 0 when they agree everywhere, 1 when they do not overlap at all.

This is the two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic applied to model scores. The reading used in this course: 0.05 is worth a look, 0.10 is an alert.

Two windows, one reference

Both windows hold 2,000 scores. The reference is normal with mean 0.50 and spread 0.10, and that curve never moves. The live mean μ is the slider; its spread stays at 0.10, so the only change is location.

At μ = 0.50 the curves coincide and D = 0. Raise μ and the live curve slides right. The figure draws densities because they are easier to read than cumulative curves, but D is measured on the cumulative versions — the single point where the two are furthest apart.

Every alert in this figure comes from a mean shift of a few hundredths of a score point. That is not a sensitive test misbehaving: D measures difference, and with 2,000 rows the difference is real. Whether it matters is a separate question, answered by the decision and the labels.

Reading the number

For two normal curves with equal spread, D has a closed form: D = 2Φ(δ / 2σ) − 1, where δ is the shift and σ the spread. At δ = 0.06 and σ = 0.10 that is 2Φ(0.30) − 1 = 0.24. Φ is the share of a normal curve below a value, which any statistics function will hand you.

The number is a distance, so it has no direction: a rise and a fall of the same size give the same D. It also saturates. Doubling the shift from 0.05 to 0.10 does not double D; it moves 0.20 to 0.38. D ranks shifts well and measures them in nothing at all, which is why a dashboard shows the two distributions beside it.

An alert opens an investigation, and the first check is the pipeline, not the model: units, ranges, missingness, duplicates. A score that quietly changed from dollars to cents produces exactly this picture, and it is a data-quality event.

The trap— D

Most common wrong mental model

"D = 0 means the model is fine." D compares score distributions. A model can keep its scores and lose accuracy, because the link between a score and an outcome changed underneath it.

Correction: treat inputs and scores as leading indicators, then confirm with accuracy once labels arrive. The alert is a question with an owner, not an instruction.

The tell: an accuracy drop with a flat D points at the labels, the cut or the outcome job. Drift in the inputs would have shown up first.

Retrieval check— D

  1. The live mean moves from 0.50 to 0.55, spread still 0.10. Is that an alert at the 0.10 line?
    Answer

    D = 2Φ(0.05 / 0.20) − 1 = 2Φ(0.25) − 1 ≈ 0.20, so yes: twice the alert line. A shift of about 0.013 is enough to cross 0.05.

  2. D has fallen to 0.02, but accuracy on last week's labelled rows dropped 8 points. What do you check?
    Answer

    Not the score distribution. Look at the labels, the decision cut and the outcome pipeline. A flat D says the scores look the same, not that they still mean the same thing.
