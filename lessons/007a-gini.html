Gini impurity — scoring a split

You can compute a node's impurity and use the drop to decide whether a split is worth taking.

Where this sits

Δ = I(parent) − (nL/n) I(L) − (nR/n) I(R), where I = 1 − Σk pk2

I is Gini; n is the total row count; pk is the share of class k

I is impurity: how mixed a node's labels are. pk is the fraction of rows in class k, and the sum runs over the classes. With two classes, shares p and 1 − p, the formula collapses to I = 2p(1 − p).

Impurity is a mixing score

A node that is all one class has p = 1, so I = 0: pure. A 50/50 node has I = 1 − (0.25 + 0.25) = 0.5, the maximum for two classes. The score is highest when you know least.

Gini is not the only impurity. Entropy plays the same role, and trees often use either. What matters here is that a split is judged by how much impurity it removes, not by how small one child looks.

Score a split by the drop

A cut sends rows left or right. Score each child with I, then weight by its share of rows: nL/n for the left, nR/n for the right. Subtract both from the parent. That difference, Δ, is the split criterion.

Take 100 rows, 50 positive and 50 negative: I(parent) = 0.5. A cut puts 45 positive and 5 negative on the left, and 5 positive and 45 negative on the right. Each child has I = 1 − (0.9² + 0.1²) = 0.18.

The children hold half the rows each, so the weighted impurity is 0.5 × 0.18 + 0.5 × 0.18 = 0.18. The drop is 0.5 − 0.18 = 0.32. That 0.32 is what the tree records for this cut.

A useless split changes nothing

Cut the same 100 rows at random and each child stays near 50/50. Both impurities are about 0.5, the weighted average is 0.5, and Δ is 0. A tree searches many cuts and keeps the largest Δ; a cut with no drop is not taken.

The search is greedy: take the best cut now, then repeat inside each child. Depth limits and minimum leaf sizes stop it before every row becomes its own pure, useless leaf.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

“Pick the side with the lowest impurity.” A child can be perfectly pure because it holds one row. That says nothing about the other 99. Comparing children without weights rewards tiny, unrepresentative leaves.

Correction: always weight each child by its share and compare the result with the parent. A 0.00 child with 1% of the rows contributes 0, but the other 99% still decides the score.

The tell: the winning split has one tiny perfect child and a large child almost as mixed as the parent. Check the weights: 0.99 × 0.5 ≈ 0.495, a drop of only 0.005.

Retrieval check

  1. A parent has Gini 0.5. Two children each have Gini 0.18 and each holds half the rows. What is the weighted impurity?
    Answer

    0.5 × 0.18 + 0.5 × 0.18 = 0.18. The drop is 0.32, and that is the number the tree compares against other cuts.

  2. A cut isolates 1 row in 100 and leaves it pure; the other 99 rows stay at Gini 0.5. Is it a good split?
    Answer

    No. Weighted impurity is 0.01 × 0 + 0.99 × 0.5 = 0.495, a drop of 0.005. The perfect child is too small to matter.
