Pretraining: prediction as supervision

You can contrast next-token and masked objectives, and explain what scale buys.

Why this matters

Pretraining is the stage where a model reads text at scale and learns one skill: predict the next token. No human labels are needed, because every document carries its own answer key, and the result is a probability distribution over text rather than a store of facts.

That single sentence explains most of what a base model can and cannot do. It can continue a sentence in a style it has seen thousands of times; it cannot look anything up, and it has no notion of being right. In an exam, "a model reports loss 3.0 nats — what is its perplexity?" separates people who know the definition from people who compute 3^e or 10³; the answer is e³ ≈ 20, and the unit is "equally likely tokens". In a project, the classic evaluation bug is comparing perplexity between two models that use different tokenizers: 50,000-token and 100,000-token vocabularies score different units, so the smaller number may belong to the worse model. The second classic is averaging loss per batch rather than per token, which lets short sequences dominate.

The one core idea

Imagine a fill-in-the-blank worksheet where the answer key is printed on the same page, under a flap. You read the sentence so far, guess the next word, lift the flap, and adjust. Nobody has to write the answers, because the text already contains them — that is what self-supervision means: the labels come from the data itself.

In one line: hide the next token, measure how much probability the model gave it, and push that probability up. With a 50,000-token vocabulary, a model that guesses uniformly has loss ln(50,000) = 10.82 nats; a trained model at loss 3.0 has perplexity e³ ≈ 20, i.e. it is as unsure as a choice among 20 tokens.

How it actually works

Seven parts, following one document through one training step.

perplexity = exp( −(1/N) Σ log p(token_t | token_<t) )

Part 1 — Turn text into token IDs

A tokenizer splits text into pieces and maps each piece to an integer, so a sentence becomes a list such as [464, 3797, 3332]. A typical vocabulary holds 50,000 entries, which fixes the size of the model's output. Why this part exists: a network can only emit numbers from a finite set, and 50,000 is small enough to compute a full distribution over at every position.

Part 2 — Make every position a training pair

Slide a window along the document: the input is tokens 1 to t, the target is token t+1. A 1,024-token window yields 1,024 separate predictions from a single pass, one per position. Why this part exists: without the one-token shift, the model would be asked to reproduce its own input, which is a copy task and teaches nothing about what comes next.

Part 3 — Produce a probability for every token

The final layer outputs one score, called a logit, for each vocabulary entry. Softmax turns those 50,000 scores into positive numbers that sum to 1, one distribution per position. Why this part exists: the loss needs the probability assigned to the correct token, and a raw score has no meaningful scale on its own.

Part 4 — Score the answer with cross-entropy

The loss at one position is −ln p(correct token). A probability of 0.5 costs 0.693 nats, 0.1 costs 2.303, and 0.01 costs 4.605 — the penalty grows without limit as confidence in the wrong answer grows. Why this part exists: a loss that merely counted mistakes would treat "almost right" and "certainly wrong" the same.

Part 5 — Average, backpropagate, repeat

The 1,024 per-position losses are averaged into one number for the step, gradients flow back through the whole block stack, and the optimiser nudges every weight. Reporting exp(loss) gives perplexity. Why this part exists: training needs a single scalar per step; a vector of 1,024 losses cannot be compared across batches or runs.

Part 6 — The masked variant

A masked model hides about 15% of positions and predicts them from both sides at once, which is how BERT is trained. It sees the whole context, so it is a strong encoder, but it never learns p(next | past). Why this part exists: tasks such as classification or extraction need the right-hand context, while generation needs the left; one objective cannot supply both.

Part 7 — Buy capability with scale

Loss falls as a power law in parameters, data and compute: a straight line on a log-log plot, with no cliff. Capability improvements follow that curve far more reliably than they follow architecture tweaks. Why this part exists: it tells you where the next unit of effort pays — more unique tokens, not a cleverer block.

Worked example

1. Reading a loss number

Perplexity is e raised to the loss, so a loss of L nats means the model is about as uncertain as a uniform choice among e^L tokens.

What to notice. A drop of one nat divides perplexity by e = 2.718, which is why leaderboard gaps of 0.05 nats are real differences and not noise. The scale is multiplicative: going from 5.0 to 3.0 is not "40% better", it is a 7.4-fold reduction in uncertainty.

Exponentiating gives 4.31, which is exactly the geometric mean of 2, 4 and 10 — the reciprocal probabilities. That is why perplexity is described as an effective number of choices, and why one very confident miss moves it more than three mild ones.

3. A bigram model on eight words of context

Count what follows in "the cat sat on the mat . the cat ate the fish . the dog sat on the mat . the dog ate the bone ." "The" occurs 8 times and "sat" twice; each probability below is a count divided by those totals.

The zero for "sat → on" is not a triumph: with two observations the model assigns probability 1, and a third continuation would be punished without limit. Counting is all a bigram can do.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"Lower perplexity means a better assistant." It feels right because perplexity is the number printed on the training curve, it always goes down, and smaller feels like an objective improvement.

The correction is that perplexity measures fit to the pretraining distribution — mostly web text, code and books — and nothing else. It says nothing about instruction following, factuality or safety, all of which arrive in post-training (topic 29). A model can be superb at predicting the next token of a forum argument and useless when asked to summarise a contract. A second trap is treating benchmark scores as independent evidence: public test sets leak into pretraining corpora, so a high score can reflect memorisation rather than skill.

The tell: the same model with a better perplexity score fails a two-line instruction test, or answers a riddle by reciting the dataset version of it. The other tell is a perplexity comparison across tokenizers or across languages — if the vocabulary or the token count per sentence changed, the number changed unit, and the comparison is meaningless.

Retrieval check

  1. A model reports loss 2.3 nats. What is its perplexity, and what does that mean?
    Answer

    e²·³ ≈ 10. At each position it is about as uncertain as a uniform choice among 10 tokens — reasonably fluent, far from solved.

  2. Why can a causal LM be used for generation but a masked LM cannot?
    Answer

    The causal model is trained on p(token | previous tokens), exactly the distribution needed to sample text. The masked model is trained to fill gaps using both sides, which is unavailable during left-to-right generation.

  3. What does self-supervision replace, and what does it cost?
    Answer

    It replaces human labels with the text itself. The cost is scale: you need enormous corpora and compute, and the objective optimises plausibility rather than truth.

Build it in Colab

Runnable, NumPy-only, no setup: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. The cell prints numbers, not pictures — change one value and rerun to see what breaks.

from collections import Counter, defaultdict
import numpy as np
text = ("the cat sat on the mat . the cat ate the fish . "
        "the dog sat on the mat . the dog ate the bone .").split()
bigram = defaultdict(Counter)
for a, b in zip(text, text[1:]):
    bigram[a][b] += 1
