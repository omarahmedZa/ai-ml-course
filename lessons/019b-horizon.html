The RL frame, one symbol at a time · Lab 2

1/(1−γ) — how far the agent looks

You can convert any γ into a number of steps, and say why 0.99 → 0.999 is a tenfold change.

Where this sits

effective horizon ≈ 1 / (1 − γ)

Topic 19 · γ is now the denominator, so small changes near 1 move the answer a long way

γ sets how fast a reward fades. This lab turns that fade rate into something you can say out loud at a design meeting: the number of steps the agent is still, in effect, looking ahead.

A count of steps

Add up every discount weight, 1 + γ + γ² + …, and the total is 1/(1−γ). That total is the count of future steps that still carry weight. At γ = 0.9 it is 10 steps; at γ = 0.99 it is 100. Nothing random is involved — this is arithmetic on the weights alone.

Why that sum works: multiply 1/(1−γ) by (1−γ) and every power cancels except the first. You do not need the algebra to use the formula, but it explains why nothing enters it except γ.

What a long horizon costs

A long horizon is not free. Every step of delay counts at nearly full value, so one lucky or unlucky episode swings the estimate. The spread of sampled returns grows with the horizon, which is why γ = 0.999 needs far more data than γ = 0.9 before two policies can be ranked reliably.

Choosing γ

Pick γ from the task, not from a library default. If the payoff arrives about 20 steps out, you want 1/(1−γ) comfortably above 20: γ = 0.95 gives 20, and 0.99 gives 100. Too small and the goal is invisible; too large and each update is mostly noise.

A quick rule: convert the candidate γ to a step count, then compare it with the number of steps your payoff actually needs. A ratio near 1 is a fragile setting.

One more example

The horizon counts steps, but γ also sets how much of a reward survives each one. Hold the step count at ten and read the discount straight off: at γ = 0.9 only about a third of the value is left after ten steps, while at γ = 0.999 almost all of it is.

Check the last two rows

For 0.8: 1 − 0.8 = 0.2, and 1 / 0.2 = 5 steps. For 0.95: 1 − 0.95 = 0.05, and 1 / 0.05 = 20 steps. The extra 0.15 of γ bought fifteen more steps, because the denominator fell to a quarter of its size.

The trap

Most common wrong mental model

"γ = 0.99 is basically γ = 1, so the difference is cosmetic." It does round to 1.00 at two decimals, and that is exactly the trap. The horizon is a reciprocal, so a change in the third decimal is a change in scale, not a rounding detail.

Correction: never compare two γ values directly. Convert both to 1/(1−γ) first, then compare the step counts.

The tell: nudge γ from 0.99 to 0.999 and your agent goes from looking 100 steps ahead to 1,000. If a knob changes the planning window by a factor of ten while appearing to move by 0.009, it is the most consequential number in the configuration.

Retrieval check

  1. What horizon does γ = 0.98 give?
    Answer

    1 / 0.02 = 50 steps. Notice that 0.98 sits much closer to 0.99 (100 steps) than to 0.9 (10 steps) — the scale is not linear in γ.

  2. Which agent still counts a reward 50 steps out: γ = 0.9 or γ = 0.99?
    Answer

    γ = 0.99. That reward keeps 0.99⁵⁰ ≈ 0.61 of face value, while at γ = 0.9 it keeps 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005. The horizon of 100 against 10 predicted that gap before you computed it.
