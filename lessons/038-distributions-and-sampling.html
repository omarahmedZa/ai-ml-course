Distributions & sampling

You can compute a standard error, read a confidence interval, and say how much a mean would move if the data were collected again.

FoundationsProbability & Statistics

Why this matters— where the noise lives

A test score is one draw from a process that could have gone differently. Report 84% accuracy without an error bar and the next run's 81% looks like a regression, though nothing changed. Every comparison in machine learning — model A against model B, this prompt against that one, today's evaluation against last week's — is a comparison of noisy numbers. Sampling theory is what turns a bare number into a claim you can defend.

The failure it prevents. Two models score 0.71 and 0.72 on 1,000 test items. The difference is one percentage point, about 10 items. With a standard error near 0.014, that gap is well inside the noise, yet a team ships the "winner" and rewrites the roadmap. The same arithmetic explains why a 20-example prototype proves nothing.

The one core idea— where the noise lives

A distribution says which values are likely and how likely. A statistic is a number computed from a sample — a mean, a proportion, an accuracy. Because the sample is random, the statistic is random too: collect the data again and it changes.

The distribution of that statistic over repeated samples is the sampling distribution. Its spread is the standard error, written SE. For a mean, SE = σ/√n(sigma), where σ (sigma) is the population standard deviation and n is the sample size. The square root is the important part: to halve the noise you need four times the data.

This is the whole machinery. A confidence interval is just an estimate plus and minus a multiple of its standard error. When you read "0.71 ± 0.03", the 0.03 is a standard error dressed for publication.

SE = σ / √n  ·  95% interval ≈ estimate ± 1.96 SE

The spread of the data shrinks as √n (square root), not as n. Precision is expensive.

How it actually works— where the noise lives

Part 1 — Describe the population with a mean and a spread

For the five values {2,4,6,8,10}, the mean is 30/5 = 6. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean: (16+4+0+4+16)/5 = 8. The standard deviation is its square root, √8 = 2.83, which returns to the original units.

Why this matters: it describes one draw, not the uncertainty of a mean — a smaller quantity, and this lesson's trap.

Part 2 — Take a sample and compute a statistic

Draw four values with replacement, say {2,4,6,10}. Their mean is 22/4 = 5.5, not 6. A second sample might give 6.5, a third 5.0. Each sample mean is one observation from the sampling distribution.

Why this matters: the estimate moves because the sample moved. Reporting a single mean without saying how much it moves presents noise as fact.

Part 3 — Predict the spread with the standard error

With σ = 2.83 and n = 4, SE = 2.83/2 = 1.41. For n = 16 it is 0.71; for n = 64, 0.35. This is the curve in the figure: same population, narrower bell.

Why this matters: it tells you what data volume buys before you collect it. Going from 1,000 to 4,000 examples halves the error bar; 1,000 to 2,000 only shrinks it by 29%.

Part 4 — Turn the error into an interval

For large samples the sampling distribution is close to normal, so about 95% of estimates fall within 1.96 standard errors of the truth. Reverse that and you get a 95% confidence interval: estimate ± 1.96 × SE. For proportions, replace σ with √(p(1−p)), giving SE = √(p(1−p)/n).

Why this matters: it is the smallest honest error bar you can put on an accuracy, a conversion rate or a benchmark score. The central limit theorem is why the bell appears even when the data are not normal.

Read it as a sentence. This sample says 5.5, and the procedure that produced it lands within 2.77 of the truth 95% of the time. The interval contains 6.0, so there is no evidence the sample is unusual. Note that the sample mean missed by 0.5, which is only 0.5 / 1.41 = 0.35 standard errors — an ordinary miss.

2. Accuracy on 100 test items

A model gets 60 of 100 items right. The estimate is p = 0.60 and SE = √(0.6 × 0.4 / 100) = √0.0024 = 0.049. The 95% interval is 0.60 ± 1.96(0.049) = 0.60 ± 0.096, or [0.504,0.696].

Second model. It scores 66 of 100, p = 0.66, SE = √(0.66 × 0.34 / 100) = 0.047. The difference is 0.06 with a combined standard error of √(0.049² + 0.047²) = 0.068, so the gap is less than one standard error. Two intervals overlap heavily, and the data cannot separate the models. That is the correct conclusion, not "B is 10% better".

3. Why 1,000 examples still give ±3%

At p = 0.7 and n = 1,000, SE = 0.0145, so the 95% half-width is about 3 points. Getting down to ±1 point needs roughly 8,000 examples: precision costs quadratically, which is why benchmark gaps under two points are unreadable at 1,000 items.

The trap— where the noise lives

Most common wrong mental model

"The standard deviation tells me how uncertain my mean is." It does not. The standard deviation describes how spread out individual observations are; the standard error describes how spread out means are. They differ by √n.

With σ = 2.83 the data really are about 2.83 wide, and that does not change with sample size. A mean from 10,000 draws has SE = 0.028, 100× smaller, so quoting 2.83 overstates the uncertainty by 100×.

Correction: error bars on a mean use s/√n. Error bars describing the spread of the data use s. Label the axis so a reader knows which one is drawn.

The tell: an error bar that stays the same width when you add 100× more data is a standard deviation, not a standard error. A second tell is an error bar wider than the possible range of the quantity — a proportion of 0.6 with a bar of 2.83 is a units mistake, not a wide estimate.

Retrieval check— where the noise lives

  1. Standard deviation 20, sample size 100. What is the standard error, and what happens to it at size 400?
    Answer

    SE = 2. At 400, SE = 1 — quadrupling the sample halved the error, because the divisor is a square root.

  2. What does "95% confidence" actually mean?
    Answer

    It describes the procedure: over many repeated samples, about 95% of intervals built this way contain the true value. It is not a 95% probability that this particular interval does.

  3. Model A scores 0.71 and B 0.72 on 1,000 items, with SE about 0.014 each. Is B better?
    Answer

    No evidence of it. The gap is 0.01 and the standard error of the difference is about 0.02, so the gap is half a standard error — the kind of difference a rerun would erase.

Build it in Colab— where the noise lives

Runnable, NumPy-only: open the notebook in Colab · local copy. It draws 10,000 sample means at three sample sizes and checks the measured spread against σ/√n.

import numpy as np
pop = np.array([2.,4.,6.,8.,10.])
means = [np.mean(np.random.choice(pop,4)) for _ in range(10000)]
print(pop.mean(),pop.std())          # 6.0 2.828
print(np.std(means))                  # about 1.41
print(pop.std() / np.sqrt(4))         # 1.414
