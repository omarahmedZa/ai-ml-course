Depth, one multiplication at a time · Lab 1

gL — how much gradient survives

You can compute how much of a gradient reaches layer 1 after L layers, and say which fix applies to which direction.

Where this sits

gradient at layer 1 = gL × gradient at the last layer

Lab 1 of 2 · the product is lit; everything else is one factor per layer

Training sends a correction signal backwards from the loss. It passes through every layer on the way, and each layer multiplies it by a number. g is that per-layer number — the gain. L is the number of layers it must cross. Nothing is added along the path, so only the product matters.

One multiplication per layer

Call the gain g. Ten layers give g10, fifty give g50. If g is a little below 1 the signal fades; a little above 1 it grows; exactly 1 it survives unchanged. Drag the slider and watch the same curve change character.

The two numbers to remember

A sigmoid layer's derivative never exceeds 0.25, so ten sigmoid layers pass 0.2510 ≈ 9.5 × 10−7 — below what a 32-bit float resolves usefully. In the other direction, a badly scaled stack with g = 1.5 passes 1.510 ≈ 58 after ten layers and 1.530 ≈ 191,751 after thirty.

Five fixes, one product

ReLU keeps the factor at 1 for positive inputs. He initialisation spreads each weight by √(2/fan_in), steadying activation scale. Normalisation re-centres every layer to mean 0, variance 1. Those three hold the factor near 1. Residuals add an identity path instead, so the factor becomes 1 + g. Clipping rescales gradients above a threshold, capping the exploding side.

The trap— gL

Most common wrong mental model

"Vanishing gradients mean the learning rate is too small." The learning rate scales one step; it cannot repair a product. At g = 0.25 the first layer receives a millionth of the signal, so a rate a thousand times larger is still a thousand times too small — and it destabilises the layers that were healthy.

Correction: measure the gradient size at each layer before touching the learning rate.

The tell: print each layer's gradient norm. If it falls by orders of magnitude from the last layer to the first, depth is the cause, not the step size. If the loss becomes NaN within a few steps, the same product is running the other way.

Retrieval check— gL

  1. A network has gain g = 0.8 and 20 layers. What fraction of the gradient reaches layer 1?
    Answer

    0.820 ≈ 0.012, about 1%. Ninety-nine percent of the correction is spent before it arrives, so the first layers barely move.

  2. Your first five layers have gradients near 10−9 while the last layer is healthy. What single change do you try first?
    Answer

    Replace sigmoid with ReLU: the per-layer factor rises from at most 0.25 to 1. Initialisation and normalisation come next. The learning rate is not the cause, because it scales one step rather than the product.
